<p class="lede">The <b>Eleven Days</b> were the spring of 1960 at <a href="../Places/Frostwick">Frostwick</a>, when a young man walking north broke the town's winter, and it thawed, for the first time in living memory, for eleven days. On the twelfth day the town held a meeting, and retained a practitioner to put the winter back on a sensible footing, and asked the young man to cut a ribbon. He has cut forty-two.</p>

## The old winter

Before 1960 Frostwick was under a winter that did not end. It was not a cold spell. It was a winter that had got stuck, and had been stuck since before anybody in the town could remember, and the town believed it to be a curse, and had built a living on it: carols, lanterns, the snow, and visitors who came a long way to stand in it. <a href="../People/Ida_Stagg">Ida Stagg</a>, who was born there in 1937, says that until she was twenty-two she had never seen a gutter run.

## Everybody went north

In the spring of 1960 the Forty-Fourth, <a href="../People/Ambrose_Fitch">Ambrose Fitch</a>, set out from <a href="../Places/Thistlewick">Thistlewick</a> for the Tower, and the whole of the south went north with him, or after him, or a little before him, on the general understanding that something was going to happen and that it would be a shame to miss it. Farmers, tinkers, a man with a goat. One of them was <a href="../People/Perce_Stagg">Percival Stagg</a>, twenty-six, a cabbage grower of Fenny Stretton, in his father's red coat.

He got as far as Frostwick square in a drift up to his waist, and could not get further, and asked what was the matter, and was told that it was a curse. He said that it did not look like a curse to him; it looked like a winter that had not been told to stop. He asked who was in charge of it. Nobody was. So he went up to the top of the town, where the cold came down from, and saw to it.

> "I wasn't, you know. Chosen. Nobody chose me. Everybody went north, that year. Farmers, tinkers, a man with a goat. I got as far as here, and the winter was in the way, so I saw to it."

What he did up there he has never said, and he says that he does not entirely know. It took most of a day and a great deal of shovelling, and at the end of it there was a noise like a door shutting a long way off, and the snow began to drip. He came down the hill at dusk. The town was standing in the square, with the snow running down the gutters, and somebody started to cheer. Ida says that she was not the first, but that she was the loudest, and that he looked at her because of the noise and went pink, and that she had decided by the time he got to the bottom of the hill.

## The eleven days

It thawed for eleven days. Mrs Marchpane, at the inn that is named for them, will tell you about every one of them whether you ring the bell or not; the card on the bell reads RING FOR SERVICE, OR FOR THE STORY OF THE THAW, and in practice they are the same thing. The short version is on a brass plate by the fire, over a line painted along the skirting board: THE ELEVEN DAYS. SNOW DEPTH DURING: THIS HIGH. The line is on the floor.

In those eleven days the gutters ran and the roofs showed, and the square turned out to be cobbled, which surprised a number of people who had lived on it all their lives. Three people planted peas. Nobody sang a carol, because there was nothing to sing it about, and nobody lit a lantern, because nobody could see the point of one, and nobody came a long way to stand in anything. By the eleventh evening the town had worked out what this meant, and on the twelfth morning it had a meeting.

## The twelfth day

The founding minutes of the Working Group on the Winter are on the table in its rooms, and I copy them as I found them:

> MINUTES OF THE WORKING GROUP ON THE WINTER. Extraordinary meeting, the twelfth day.
>
> Item 1. That the winter has stopped. Noted.
>
> Item 2. Takings, carols and lanterns, since: nil. Noted, with concern.
>
> Item 3. That a practitioner be retained to put it back, on a sensible footing. For, 41. Against, 3, who had planted peas. Carried.
>
> Item 4. That Mr Stagg be asked to open the first Festival. Carried unanimously, with one abstention: Mr Stagg.
>
> Item 5. Any other business. The peas. Deferred.

The minutes were taken by Wilhelmina Thorley, nineteen, who kept the books for her father, the town's accountant, who took the chair that morning and kept it until 1979, when she took it from him. She is <a href="../People/Wilhelmina_Blagg">Mrs Blagg</a> now. The peas are still deferred.

<div class="marginalia">Mr Stagg was not consulted about putting the winter back. I asked him whether he minded. He said that a winter on a sensible footing is a very different thing from a winter that has got stuck, and that he had only ever had a quarrel with the second sort. I asked him what had stuck it in the first place. He said that nobody had ever asked, and that he expected it was the same as most things, and that somebody had simply never been told to stop. - H.B.</div>

## The first Festival

The practitioner was retained, the winter was put back, four inches overnight and not on market days, and in the spring of 1961 Frostwick held its first Winter Festival, on the twelfth, with a ribbon at noon. Mr Stagg cut it, and the town cheered, and he went pink. He married Ida the next day. The ribbon has been cut and tied so many times since that it is mostly knots, and lives in a gilt case at the Working Group's rooms: THE RIBBON. 40 FESTIVALS. 1 RIBBON. There are forty-two now.

The winter is kept now by Miss Eudora <a href="../People/Pargeter">Pargeter</a>, who is not an ice witch, and who does refrigeration, and who does January in an afternoon. Her Schedule of Snowfalls provides, for Festival Day, a light dusting, commencing at the ribbon, to settle on Mr Stagg's hat. It does.

## Since

The Eleven Days are the only spring Frostwick has had since anybody can remember, and it was eleven days long, and the town has never once wanted another. The winter is first in the register of assets, depreciating at four per cent a year, towards spring, eventually, which has been budgeted for. The inn is still the Eleven Days. Mr Stagg is sixty-eight, and still does the lanterns, and still does the face. Ida ties a plain blue ribbon to the crown on the post every year, under his, a little lower, with nothing written on it.
