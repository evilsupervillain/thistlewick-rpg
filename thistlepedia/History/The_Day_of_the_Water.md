<p class="lede">The <b>Day of the Water</b> is what the plaque at <a href="../Places/Ashcombe">Ashcombe</a> calls the Tuesday in 1989 when the lake came up over the old village. Every soul in it walked up the hill with the kettles, by a plan that one man had carried in his coat for twenty-eight years, and was out by dinner and dry by tea, and by teatime had decided to build the whole thing again, only better. It did. Ashcombe regards the flood as the best thing that ever happened to it, and has a dinner about it every year.</p>

## The valley

Old Ashcombe stood in the bottom of a valley in the east, which had always been wet. It had a church, a school, an inn called the Anchor, a maypole and a street, and one drain, in the high street, under a stone carved DRAIN. The stone is the first exhibit in the Drainage Museum now, and the card beside it says, kindly, *It was not up to it.* The blackboard in the new school gives the history of the village in full: 1. IT WAS THERE. 2. IT WAS WET. 3. WE MOVED IT.

<a href="../People/Hubert_Weir">Hubert Weir</a> was born in 1936 three doors from the drain, and stood on the stone at six in a rainstorm and watched the water go round it, and knew. He went away and learned drainage and came back, and told the parish for thirty years that the valley would flood, and the parish said that it had always been wet. In 1961 he drew a plan of what to do on the day, and put it in his coat, and it was in his coat every day for twenty-eight years.

## The Tuesday

It rained, in 1989, and went on raining, and on a Tuesday the water in the bottom of the valley stopped going anywhere and began to come up. Mr Weir took the plan out of his coat.

> "Oh, the flood. It was a Tuesday. We all went up the hill with the kettles, and by teatime we'd decided to build it again, only better."

That is Mrs Ash, and it is the account of the day that everybody in Ashcombe gives, nearly word for word, because it is the true one. They went up the hill in the order on the plan, which was by streets, and took the kettles, which were not on the plan but which everybody took regardless, and the church bell, which came up on a cart, because nobody saw why it shouldn't. The schoolmistress brought the strongbox of reports up as far as the schoolroom door, decided that it was safer where it was, and left it there in oilcloth, and she was right. The water came up over the street and the Anchor and the school, and up the church to its eaves, and stopped there, and has stayed.

## The Roll

The Roll was taken on the hill, in the rain, in the schoolmistress's hand: every name in the old village, a tick by each, and after every tick one word, DRY. There are two hundred and six ticks. Beside one name, in place of a tick, in the same careful hand: *Away. (Written to.)*

That one was Corvin's. He was nineteen, and in a corner of the Slain Wyvern at Nether Sopping, being away, on purpose. Two hundred and six people stood on a hill and counted, and when they got to him they wrote down where he was, and then they wrote to him.

> "A tremendous success. Everybody out by dinner, everybody dry by tea, and not so much as a hen lost. We have the Roll."

## Teatime

The decision was made on the hill, at about four, over tea, from the kettles. There was no meeting. Mr Weir says that somebody said it would be a shame to lose the name, and somebody else said that you could see your hand in front of your face up here, which you never could in the old kitchen, and that by the second cup everybody was agreed. They would build it again, only better, with the light, and with drains.

They built it on the shore, eight miles from the old village by the road, which goes all the way round the lake, and a stone's throw by any other measure. Everything is new. The church is pale stone with a green roof and the old bell in the tower, and it rings at six for dinner. Mr Weir drained it: fourteen hundred yards of culvert, a fall of one in two hundred, and not one puddle in Ashcombe since the day they went in.

Corvin took the name Ash, after the village, in its memory. His mother took it too, to save the postman.

## Remembered

The plaque faces the lake, with the gilt still bright:

<div class="stamp">HERE, ON THE DAY OF THE WATER, EVERY SOUL IN OLD ASHCOMBE CAME SAFE UP THE HILL, AND WAS DRY BY TEATIME. WE BUILT IT AGAIN, ONLY BETTER.<br>MIND THE DRAINS. THEY ARE VERY GOOD. VISIT THE MUSEUM! (FREE.)</div>

The Drainage Museum has an empty plinth, beautifully polished: THE FLOOD (ON LOAN TO THE LAKE). In one front room a line is painted on the wall at knee height, with the date beside it, and a smiley face.

There is a **Flood Dinner** every year in the church hall, in sluice week, with soup, a fish, a pudding and speeches, and the toast is TO THE WATER, WHICH WAS VERY GOOD ABOUT IT. The speaker has been, in order, the Curator, the Curator, Mrs Ash on knitting, the Curator and the Curator. In the year 2000 it was Corvin Ash, who gave the village its name back, and in 2001 it was the Curator, on culverts, for some time. The papers of the Flood Dinner Committee are kept in what was Corvin's bedroom; Hon. Sec., Mrs Ash.

Once a year, in the same week, the lake is let down for the sluices, and the old street comes up for air. The bell, they say, can be heard ringing under the water on a still day. It cannot. It is up the road, calling somebody in for their dinner. They took it with them.

<div class="marginalia">I have been to the Flood Dinner, in 2001, as Mrs Ash's guest, and I have heard the toast drunk by most of the two hundred and six who are on the Roll as DRY, and by the one who is on it as Away, and was early. I have been to a good many commemorations in my time, and this is the only one I have ever been to at which nobody was sorry about anything, and I have thought about it since more than about any of the others. - H.B.</div>
