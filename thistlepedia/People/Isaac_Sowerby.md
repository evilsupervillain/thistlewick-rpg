<p class="lede"><b>Isaac Sowerby</b>, Old Sowerby to the whole of <a href="../Places/Upper_Clanging">Upper Clanging</a>, gave the <a href="../Institutions/The_Hoyle_Works">Hoyle Works</a> sixty years, and started on the rivets, and finished on the rivets. He is the town's memory. He is entirely cheerful about what he remembers, which is a great many airships going up and the same number coming down, and he sits on a bench in the street, in a knit cap, and will tell you about it.</p>

## Sixty years

> "Sixty years I gave that works. Started on the rivets. Finished on the rivets."

He went into the works at fourteen, in the autumn of 1939, as an apprentice, with a number. The works has numbered its apprentices since 1804, and Miss Hoyle's present apprentice, Nib, is the fortieth. Of the thirty-nine before her, thirty-three left, and six stayed, and one of them is Sowerby. He was put on the rivets in his first week, because somebody had to be, and he found that he liked it, and asked to stay on them, and he did, until the autumn of 1999. He says that he was offered other work, several times, and that he always asked what was wrong with the rivets, and nobody could tell him.

> "I have seen a hundred and ninety-nine of them go up. I have seen the same number come down. Never in a hurry about it, mind. They come down like a man sitting."

I am bound to say, as a man who counts in plain years, that the first of the hundred and ninety-nine went up a long time before he was born. I raised this with him. He said that he had seen the drawings. Then he said that he had riveted forty of them himself and could tell you where every rivet went, and that a man who has done that has seen the others too, near enough, and I found I did not want to argue.

> "It is the coming down that wants the work. She will get to it."

*She* is Miss Hoyle. He says it the way other men say the sun will come up.

## The benches

There are two benches in the street outside the works. One has a brass plate, FOR SOWERBY. SIXTY YEARS ON THE RIVETS, which the works put up when he retired. He sits on the other one. He says that his is for visitors. If you sit on his, he will nod at you, and you will feel welcome, which is, I think, the idea.

## The snap tin

In 1989 his snap tin went south in a carrier's crate nobody claimed, and he did not see it again for eleven years. In the year 2000 <a href="Nix">Nix</a> brought it back to him, as a return and not a location, with the sandwich still in it. He opened it, and regarded the sandwich with respect, and said:

> "Dripping. She always did me dripping."

He did not say anything else about who she was, and I did not ask. He held out a rivet, for Nix's trouble. Nix said that she could not, because it went in the book as returned and not as sold. He said, "It's a rivet. You cannot beat a rivet." I am told that she took it in the end, and that it is not in the book at all.

<div class="marginalia">In the Long Field, among the things in the grass, there is a single rivet with a ring of small white stones round it, so that nobody treads on it. The stones are Mr Sowerby's. Miss Hoyle's memorandum says that he will not be told to move them and nobody has tried. I asked him which attempt the rivet was off. He said that it was off one of them, and that it did not matter which, and that it was a good rivet, and he had put it in. - H.B.</div>

## Afterwards

He is seventy-six. In the year 2000 he saw the two hundredth go up, and he was, I am told, the only man in Upper Clanging who did not cheer. He was watching for the coming down. When she came down, beside the Tower, in her own time, he said that it was very well done, in the voice other men keep for a rivet, and went back to his bench. He still sits on the other one. The two hundred and first's keel was laid in 2001, and he has been down to look at the rivets, and has said that they will do.
