<p class="lede"><b>Mr Plummer</b> keeps the haulage yard in the north-west corner of <a href="../Places/Great_Dunnage">Great Dunnage</a>, a broad man in a brown coat, with a moustache, who does anything, anywhere, by Tuesday. He does it with a partner. The partner is the grey. It says so on the sign, in a different paint and a different hand, in case anybody should think that he had meant it as a figure of speech.</p>

## Anything, anywhere, by Tuesday

He introduces the firm and himself in one breath, and in that order:

> "Plummer. Haulage. Anything, anywhere, by Tuesday."

He was born in Great Dunnage in 1944, and the yard was his father's, and for thirty years the sign over the gate said PLUMMER. HAULAGE., and the firm was him and a cart and whatever horse he could hire. Great Dunnage has nine works, and all of them want something taken somewhere, and most of what they want taken is going to Sump, and he has taken it there, by Tuesday, since he was nineteen.

I asked him why Tuesday. He said that you have to say a day, or people stand in your yard and ask, and that Monday is too soon to be honest and Wednesday is too late to be any use. Then he said that it was not his department any longer. "She plans the Tuesdays."

## The partner

One winter, in 1994, a grey mare walked into his yard off the west road on her own, with a good saddle on, and nobody in it. He put her in a stall and waited for whoever had lost her. "Nobody came for her. So she stayed." She was the best worker in the yard inside a month. She knows every road in the valley, and had, he thinks, been taught to wait at a gate for somebody who did not come out, because she will stand at any gate in the valley for as long as it takes, and nothing hurries her, and in haulage that is worth more than speed.

So she has a stall with her name on it, and a say in the routes. "It seemed only fair." He gave her the name himself. There was a crest on the saddle, a knight in a field, looking back over his shoulder, and Mr Plummer looked at the crest and then at the mare, who was looking at him as a partner in a business looks at a customer, and called her Duchess. "It seemed to suit her." The sign went up the next spring, and under it, in a different paint and a different hand, which he says was a sign-painter's, at his request: (THE PARTNER IS THE GREY.)

## The saddle

He kept the saddle for six years, oiled, in the stable, against the day somebody came. In the year 2000 somebody did. <a href="Sir_Aldric_Pemberton-Gore_III">Sir Aldric Pemberton-Gore</a> came into the yard with the Forty-Eighth's party, and Mr Plummer looked at the tabard for rather a long time: a knight on it, in a field, facing the wrong way.

> "Begging your pardon, sir. That crest."

He brought out the saddle, and Sir Aldric said that it was his saddle, and that he had had a horse, and that he had tied her up in a village with a sign and gone to find the gate. Duchess lifted her head from the road for the first time anybody could remember, and looked at him, and he crossed the yard to her, and she put her nose in the front of his tabard, where it always went. When it was possible to say anything, Mr Plummer asked what he had called her. "Do you know, I only ever called her Horse." "Duchess, then," said Mr Plummer, and Sir Aldric said yes, it suited her.

Then Mr Plummer said the hardest thing I think he has ever had to say, which was that she would be Sir Aldric's, of course, saddle and all, only she did the canal run, and the Tuesdays, and - and he stopped, because he is a tactful man. He did not need to go on. Sir Aldric looked at her, and said, *Oh, no. No, no. Look at her. She is doing so well.* He asked whether he might visit. Not on a Tuesday. "Any day but a Tuesday, sir. She'd like that."

<div class="marginalia">The fair copy of the first half of this history went to my printer by Plummer &amp; Partner, in a crate, in the summer of 2001, and arrived on the Tuesday. I had asked for it to go by the road I knew. It went by a road I had never heard of, which Mr Plummer told me afterwards was the partner's choice, and which was four miles shorter and had no fords in it, and the crate arrived dry. The invoice was made out to me from both of them, and is signed by Mr Plummer, and beside his signature there is a mark, in pencil, of a horseshoe. He says that she likes to see the paperwork. - H.B.</div>

## Afterwards

The sign inside the yard gate has a third line now, under the second, in a third hand, very neat: (HER KNIGHT CALLS. NOT TUESDAYS.) Mr Plummer will not say whose hand it is. He says that it is the partner's business. Sir Aldric calls whenever he is in the valley, and tells her about his week, and she listens, and goes on considering the Tuesdays. Mr Plummer has put a chair by her stall, for callers, and has never sat in it.

The saddle is on the wall of the stable, oiled, over her stall. It is Sir Aldric's, and she is his, of course. Nobody has moved either of them.
