<p class="lede"><b>Gudgeon Cotterill</b> is the third of the ten Cotterills of <a href="../Places/Upper_Clanging">Upper Clanging</a>, and clerks at the Parish Rooms, where she copies the register in the best hand Mrs Tunnicliffe has had in eleven clerks. She has written <i>Cotterill</i> more times than anybody alive, and spelt it right every time, which she says is more than can be said for anybody else. She knew which drawer Form P-9 was in without looking, although nobody had ever asked for one, and she read it to the Forty-Eighth one section at a time, and wished him good luck, and meant it, roughly.</p>

## The register

She went to the Parish Rooms at fifteen, in 1995, to help Mrs Tunnicliffe with the census, and stayed, because she was good at it and because it was the only room in Upper Clanging with nobody else in it from her family. She copies the register: the births, the marriages and the deaths, in ink, in a braid and spectacles, in a hand that Mrs Tunnicliffe says is the best she has ever had. Mrs Tunnicliffe has had eleven.

"Cotterill," she told the Forty-Eighth, "hundred and twelve times." Then she gave him the family's ages, out of the register, all at once, which is a thing she does for visitors whether they ask or not:

> "It is all in here. Cotter is twenty-four. Rivet is twenty-two. I am twenty. Tappet eighteen, Ferrule sixteen, Grommet fifteen, Clevis thirteen, Shim eleven, Spare nine. And Bessie is a Cotterill now, which is another one to write."

And then, after a pause:

> "You have gone quiet. Everybody goes quiet just there."

They do. I did. It is a great many Cotterills to have read out to you in one breath, and she knows it, and she has, I think, a quiet relish for it, the way a man who keeps bees enjoys telling you how many.

<div class="marginalia">She would like it recorded, and I record it: <i>two t's and two l's. Everybody gets it wrong.</i> I got it wrong in my notes, and she corrected them, in ink, in the best hand Mrs Tunnicliffe has had. I have kept the page. - H.B.</div>

## Form P-9

When Spare applied to go with the Forty-Eighth, it was Gudgeon who told him there was a form for it, P-9, *Application by a Minor to Accompany a Chosen One (Parish of Upper Clanging)*, and that nobody had ever asked for one, and that it wanted a parent or guardian, signed, in ink. "And Mam signs it, or nobody does." Then she said, "Good luck," which is the first time I have heard anybody wish a person good luck in the voice you use for *you will need it*.

She read it out section by section. *Section one. The applicant has been heard in full.* "You have not heard him in full. He has more. He always has more." *Section two. The applicant's family has been met. All of it.* "It is a long section. Mam has the list. So do I." *Section three. Means of travel.* "He is nine. He is not walking to a tower through the Gloamwood." And then, looking at nobody in particular, and particularly at the Forty-Eighth: "If somebody had a way of getting there that was not walking, it would be different."

It was the most helpful thing anybody said to him in the north, and she said it as if she were reading out the minutes. I have asked her whether she meant it to help. She said that she reads out what is on the form.

## The column

On the morning of <a href="Washer_Cotterill">Washer</a>, in the spring of 2000, she entered the tenth Cotterill in the register before anybody else in the town knew there was one, in ink, at a quarter to seven. "Washer Cotterill. Entered in the register this morning, in ink. Two t's and two l's. Everybody gets it wrong." Then: "Ten. The column was full. I have had to rule another one."

I have seen the page. The new column is ruled with a ruler, and is the straightest line in the parish register of Upper Clanging, which goes back to 1748, and has had a good many clerks.

## Afterwards

She is still at the Parish Rooms, and is now the clerk, Mrs Tunnicliffe having become, in 2001, the clerk's assistant, at her own request, so that she could do the urns. She has entered <a href="Bessie_Cotterill">Bessie</a>'s baby, in a column of its own. She has begun, in the evenings, a fair copy of the whole of the register from its first page, which is 1748, because the early hands were bad, she says, and somebody ought to, and she is up to 1790. She says that there were a great many Cotterills then as well, and that they could not spell it either.
