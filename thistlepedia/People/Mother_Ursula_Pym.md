<p class="lede"><b>Mother Ursula Pym</b> (1659-1744) was the founder of the <a href="../Institutions/The_Order_of_Whatever_Works">Order of Whatever Works</a> and of its mother house at <a href="../Places/Weeping_Bottom">Weeping Bottom</a>, which she chose in 1702, for the sea air, on the advice of every physician she consulted, none of whom had been. She wrote the Order's Rule of Life, which is four lines long, and she wrote it, by her own account, in the time it takes a kettle to boil, and then spent forty-two years being asked whether that was all, and saying yes.</p>

## Sallow Cross

She was born Ursula Pym in 1659 at Sallow Cross, a dry little market town in the exact middle of the island, the daughter of a chandler who sold candles, rope and remedies, and could not have told you which of the three did the most good. As a girl she kept the remedies shelf. By fourteen she had noticed that customers who were given a glass of water and a chair while they waited for their remedy very often went home without it, and she told her father so, and he told her to stop doing it, because it was bad for trade.

She had a chest, all her life: not a bad one, but a chest. The physicians of Sallow Cross, of whom there were four, each prescribed something different for it, and each was very confident, and she took all four remedies in turn and kept a little book of how she felt afterwards. The book survives, at Weeping Bottom. Every entry ends the same way: *Then I had a glass of water and sat down, and felt better. I do not think it was the remedy.*

<div class="marginalia">I have seen the little book. It is the earliest clinical trial I know of on this island, and its findings have never been overturned. The Sisterhouse keeps it beside Sister Bewick's eleven volumes, and I am told that it is very nearly as long as volume eleven. - H.B.</div>

## For the air

In 1701, at forty-two, she resolved to found a house of healing, and asked every physician she could find where it ought to be. They were unanimous. The sea air, they said, was the finest cure for the chest in the world. None of them had been to the sea. Neither had she. She bought a map, and found the nearest piece of coast with a valley in it, so that the house would be sheltered, and went.

The valley was the hollow between the rock and the sea, which is where the fog comes in off the water and settles, and stays. It was drizzling when she arrived, in the spring of 1702. It has been drizzling since. She stood at the top of the valley path, I am told, with her bag, and looked down into it for a long time, and then she said, "Well, it is certainly air," and went down.

She did not move the house. She was asked to, a great many times, by the Sisters, by the patients, and once by a bishop, who had come to see her about something else and caught a cold. She said that she had been sent there by every physician consulted, and that she would not like to contradict them all at once, and that in any case the patients were getting better, which was the point. They were. Nobody could say why. She said that she could, and handed them a glass of water.

The founder's stone at the gate is her wording, and the line under it, *None of whom had been*, is not. Nobody knows who added it. It is in a newer hand, and the Sisters say it was a patient, and the patients say it was a Sister, and I have a feeling, from the shape of the letters, that it was cut after 1744 by somebody who had known her and thought she would have laughed. She would have. She laughed, the house records say, "frequently, and at length, generally at physicians".

## The Rule of Life

<div class="verse">
<p>I. Drink a glass of water.<br>
II. Sit down.<br>
III. Dry socks: two pairs, and a spare.<br>
IV. Then see how you feel.</p>
<span class="attribution">Mother Ursula Pym, 1702</span>
</div>

She wrote it in her first week in the valley, on the first page of a pamphlet of four, stitched, because that was the size of paper she had. The other three pages are blank, and were left blank on purpose, so that anybody who thought the Rule needed adding to would have to look at three empty pages first and see how they felt. Nobody has added anything in three hundred years. It was nearly done, once, by the symposia, and the symposia have been discontinued.

The third line has been more discussed than the other three together. It is generally agreed now that it was written in the valley, in the first week, in the drizzle, by somebody who had arrived with one pair.

## The window

The chapel glass at Weeping Bottom shows her holding up a pair of socks like a holy relic. They are dry. It is the only dry thing in any window in the Order. The glazier, a man from the coast called Ezra Mapp, put it in in 1746, two years after her death, and the Sisters paid him in soup, which he said was the best bargain he had ever made, and he had been paid in money.

They are said to be her own socks, the spare pair, which she kept dry in a tin box under her bed for forty-two years against an emergency, and never needed, because she always had the other two. The tin is in the Sisterhouse. It is empty. The Prioress says that the socks are in the window, in a manner of speaking, and that it would be a very poor kind of order that went looking for them.

## Her death

She died in the Sisterhouse on the eleventh of November 1744, at the age of eighty-five, having drunk a glass of water and sat down. The Sister who was with her, Sister Agnes Lark, asked her how she felt, and she said, "Better, thank you," which were her last words, and which the Order regards as the finest thing anybody has ever said on the subject, and puts on its cards.

She is buried at the top of the valley path, where she first stood and looked down into it, which is the only place in Weeping Bottom that the fog does not reach. On a clear day, which there have been, you can see the sea from it, and the valley full of cloud below, like a bowl of soup.

## Afterwards

The Order is one again, or two that agree; the Prioress says it is a question for a symposium, and that there will not be one. The pamphlet is back in its drawer. The Rule of Life is a by-law of the parish of Sump, and the parish walked it in the year 2000, down the street and back, and saw how it felt, and felt better. Mother Ursula's name is on the stone at the gate and in the chapel glass, and in Sump's Standing Orders, and on the card in every Sister's pocket, and on a plaque at Sallow Cross, put up in 1902 by the chandler's great-great-great-grandson, which reads: SHE WAS RIGHT ABOUT THE WATER. HE WAS WRONG ABOUT THE TRADE.
