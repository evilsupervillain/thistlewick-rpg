<p class="lede"><b>Mother Selvidge</b> reads the cards in a little striped booth at the back of a fair in the sand of the far south, a long way from anywhere, the way fairs always are, for two crowns, and has never once been wrong. Ask anybody she has read. She tells about four young men a year that they are the rival of a great hero. They all go and do something.</p>

## The road

She was born in 1922 in a wagon on the road between two fairs, the seventh child of a fairground family, and says that she was reading the cards before she could read, and that she was reading people before she could read the cards, and that the cards are only there so that people have something to look at while she reads them. She took the booth over from her aunt in 1946, and has had it since, and the sign has never changed, because she has never once had to change it.

## The reading

To the Forty-Eighth, in the year 2000:

> "You're going to go a long way, and meet a great many people, and at the end of it somebody is going to be very surprised to see you. And you'll eat a turnip. That one's free."

He went a long way, and met a great many people, and at the end of it somebody was very surprised to see him, and he ate a turnip, on the way home. I have checked. She was right about all four. She says that she generally is, and that the turnip was a gift, because a Chosen One from <a href="../Places/Thistlewick">Thistlewick</a> who does not eat a turnip on the way home has not been read properly.

## The rival

In 1989, at a fair, she told a young man of nineteen, sat in a heap, that he was the rival of a great hero, for two crowns. He went and sat in a corner of the <a href="../Places/The_Slain_Wyvern">Slain Wyvern</a> for eleven years, waiting for the hero. In the year 2000 he came back and asked her again. She did not remember him. She read him again, and got a long life, a useful trade, something with his hands, and no destiny whatsoever. He asked her to do it again. "Same again? It'll be the same again, dear. Keep your money."

Then she put the cards down and told him, kindly and without mystery:

> "Shall I tell you a thing, dear? The rival of a great hero. I say that to about four young men a year. Lads like you, at nineteen, sat in a heap. It's the reading that makes them stand up straight. And it has never once been wrong. They all went and did something."

He said that he had sat in a corner for eleven years. She said: "And then you got up. Look at you. You're stood in a fair a long way from anywhere with your friends."

<div class="marginalia">I asked her how many young men she had told they were the rival of a great hero. She said about four a year for fifty-six years, which comes to two hundred and twenty-four, and that she knew where about two hundred of them were, because they write, and that every one of them had gone and done something, and that one of them was a Mr Rook, who keeps a league table in the west and is fourth on it by his own choice, and that she was very proud of him too. - H.B.</div>

## Afterwards

She is seventy-nine. She is still in the booth, at the back of the fair, and the sign still says NEVER WRONG, and she still is. Since 2000 a great many more people have come to the fair, because the road to the Tower is busy, and she has read a great many of them, and told them that they will go a long way and meet a great many people, and she says that this is true of everybody walking to see a chair, and that it is the easiest money she has ever made, and that she has not put the price up, because the cards do not do credit, and nor do they do profiteering.
