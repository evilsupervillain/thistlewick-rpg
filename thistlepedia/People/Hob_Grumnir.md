<p class="lede"><b>Hob Grumnir</b> is the blacksmith of <a href="../Places/Thistlewick">Thistlewick</a>, third of that forge, and an enormous man who talks to his hammers. Two of the hammers have names. One of them, he insists, talks back. She is called <b>Beatrice</b>, and she joined the Forty-Eighth's company on the same morning he did, because, as he explained, "Beatrice comes. I come with Beatrice. That's the arrangement and it's not up for discussion, mainly because she'd win."</p>

## The forge

Hob learned the trade from his aunt, Beatrice Grumnir, who was born in 1919, kept the Thistlewick forge for most of the century, and died in 1989. By every account in the village she was right about everything and never once said anything anybody wanted to hear. Her headstone is in the churchyard. It now has a good gate in front of it.

He has made a very great many things and owns almost none of them, because when somebody needs a thing he makes it and says "take it", and the record of what is owed is a shelf. Half the village has something of his. When he asked for a few things back, for a competition, every one of them was glad to see him and none of them would give it up, and each of them had a better reason than the last, and he said that was fair.

## Beatrice

Beatrice is a hammer. I want to be precise about this, because I am a historian. She is a forging hammer of the ordinary kind, well used and well kept, and she is right about everything.

She told him the Forty-Seventh had come in for a sword, and got a good one, and it had not helped. She told him a sword is no good on its own, and that somebody has to swing it who knows what breaks. When the company was full she said that was a shame; "Beatrice says a lot of things." She said nothing for a fortnight once, which Hob said was not sulking, because hammers do not sulk; it was her being professional about it.

I asked in the village about Beatrice, and everybody I asked told me the same thing, which is that Beatrice was always right. Nobody in Thistlewick has ever said anything more than that, and nor shall I.

## On the Quest

**The Assize of Hammers.** <a href="../Institutions/The_Worshipful_Company_of_Smiths">The Worshipful Company of Smiths</a> at <a href="../Places/Great_Dunnage">Great Dunnage</a> judges work every three years, and Hob had never entered, having nothing he owned to enter. In 2000 he entered Beatrice, who is not his work. She was disqualified under clause one. The Wardens' notes, which I have seen, say that she is right about everything. He was entirely satisfied.

**The gate.** Beatrice went quiet, and he admitted at last that the last thing she had told him to do, eleven years before, was make a marker, and that he had never been able to get the words right. He made three markers, each one magnificent, and rejected all three ("It's right. It's not her."), and then made the churchyard a gate with a good latch, because the old one stuck and she would have hated the fuss. Beatrice began talking again on the walk home, and said the latch wants a spring.

**<a href="../Artefacts/The_Sky_Anvil">The Sky Anvil</a>.** A thing fell out of the sky into <a href="Elias_Meech">Elias Meech</a>'s top field in 1749 and has been a gatepost since. Mr Meech would not sell it, it being a very good gatepost, but he would swap it for a gate, and Hob had lately become good at gates. The star-iron made exactly one sword. Hob has not said who it is for.

**The anvil.** His own anvil was stolen, once, by two apprentice bandits on <a href="Meredith_Crooke">Meredith Crooke</a>'s six-week syllabus. They got it eleven feet, in the dark. He was angry for nine seconds and then impressed. Their apology is framed in the smithy, next to nothing he made.

**The sword at the door.** <a href="Roland_Fairweather">Roland Fairweather</a>'s sword, which Roland left at the tower door, has a maker's mark on it. Hob looked at it for some time. "That's my aunt's. She made this here. On this. Forty years back, near enough. Sold it to a man going south." And then: "She did good work." Beatrice, on that occasion, said nothing at all. He later put a Thistlewick haft on it, so that it would swing for a Thistlewick hand. "Still hers. Handle's mine."

## Bryd Ollerenshaw

<a href="Bryd_Ollerenshaw">Bryd Ollerenshaw</a>, of Ollerenshaw's at Upper Clanging, was apprenticed alongside him, and they have been rivals ever since. He won Best in Hall at the Smiths' Hall in 1987, the year he did not enter, having a cold. They met again in 2000, and went for a drink, and are still arguing about rivets. Later the same year they made the tube plate for the <a href="../History/The_Flight_of_the_Two_Hundred">Two Hundred</a>'s refit at Ollerenshaw's, in one heat, by four hands, which neither of them has mentioned since. <a href="Ott_Hoyle">Miss Hoyle</a> says it will do. One of the Cotterill children says they do this every time.

I record that they are rivals. Both of them have read this sentence and both of them asked me to leave it exactly as it is.

## Afterwards

He is at the forge. The churchyard gate swings well, and the latch has a spring now. He still has not said who the star-iron sword is for. I have a theory, and I am not allowed to print it.

In 2001 he and Bryd Ollerenshaw sat as joint judges of the Smiths' Hall prize at Great Dunnage, and disagreed for nine hours. The prize was awarded. Neither of them will say to whom.

<div class="marginalia">I read this article to him at the forge. He listened to all of it and said it was fair. Then he said that Beatrice says I have spelled Ollerenshaw right and the rest of it is soft. I have let it stand.</div>
