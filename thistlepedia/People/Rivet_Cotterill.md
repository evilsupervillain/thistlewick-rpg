<p class="lede"><b>Rivet Cotterill</b> is the second of the ten Cotterills of <a href="../Places/Upper_Clanging">Upper Clanging</a>. He works days at the foundry while his father works nights, so that they pass at the front door at six every morning, one going out and one coming in, and that is their conversation, and he is entirely satisfied with it. He has shared a bed his whole life. He was twenty-two, in the year 2000, and had never once been in a room by himself.</p>

## Days

He went into the foundry at fourteen, in 1992, on days, because Da was on nights and Mrs Cotterill said that one of them on each was the only way the house would ever have a man awake in it at all times, which she felt it needed, for the lifting. He is on the moulding floor, where the sand is, and is good at it, and says so, once, and then does not mention it again for a year.

He gave me his whole day in four sentences, which is two more than he gives most people:

> "Days. Da does nights. We pass at the door at six. Rivet. Da."

> "That is a conversation, that is. Some mornings he says the weather."

I have stood at the door at six, by arrangement, and watched it. Mr Cotterill came up the street and Rivet came out of the house, and they met on the step, and Mr Cotterill said "Rivet," and Rivet said "Da," and they went past each other, and Mr Cotterill went in, and Rivet went down the hill. Then Mr Cotterill put his head out of the door again and said, "Close," and went back in, and Rivet called "Aye" up the street without looking round. Rivet told me afterwards that this was a very full morning, and that I had been lucky, and that it was the weather.

## The bed

The house at the end of the street has three bedrooms and, until 1999, eleven people. Rivet has shared a bed, he says, his whole life, and one of the people in it is Grommet, and Grommet sleeps with his elbows. "I am twenty-two and I have never once been in a room by myself." He did not say it as a complaint. He said it as a fact, the way you might say you had never been to the sea, with a little wonder in it.

When Cotter married and moved out, in 1999, there was a vacancy in Rivet's bed, and it was filled within the hour by Clevis, who had been waiting for it, and had a book on who would get it, and had backed himself.

## Half an hour

Every evening after his shift he goes into the Safety Valve, where Mr Kell keeps a stool at the end of the bar that is understood to be his, and has a half, for half an hour, by the town clock, which has been four minutes fast since 1841, so that he is always home four minutes early, and is the only Cotterill who ever is. "Half an hour. Then bed. Then it is Tuesday." It is not always Tuesday. He says it is generally Tuesday, near enough, and that he has found it a great help to think so.

## Spring

On the morning of Washer, Mr Cotterill came off nights for it, for the first time in twenty-two years, and he and Rivet passed at the door at six, the both of them going in. "Half an hour," Rivet said. "Then bed. Then it is the spring." I asked him whether they had said anything at the door, that morning. He said that his father had said, "Rivet," and he had said, "Da," and then they had both stood there, because neither of them was going anywhere, and it had been very strange, and they had gone in.

## Afterwards

He is still on days, and still passes his father at six; Cotter is on days now as well, and passes with him, so that the conversation has become *Rivet. Cotter. Da*, which he says is a lot to take in at that hour. He is twenty-four.

In the summer of 2001 the whole of the family went to Bessie's to see the baby, on a Sunday, and Rivet came home early, from the foundry's cricket, and found the house empty, and his bed empty, and the whole of the room his own, for the first time in his life. He lay down in it. He says that it was very quiet, and very wide, and that he lay there for some time and looked at the ceiling. Then he went downstairs and sat in the kitchen with the door open until Grommet came home, and then he went up to bed.
