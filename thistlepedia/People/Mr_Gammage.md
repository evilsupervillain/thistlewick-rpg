<p class="lede"><b>Obadiah Gammage</b> of <a href="../Places/Sump">Sump</a> sat against a wall with his eyes shut and was not asleep, and had been trying to get off since Tuesday, which in Sump was a condition of the curse. In the year 2000, as the eldest, he spoke the parish's riddle, and the curse was lifted, and the parish adopted Mother Ursula Pym's Rule of Life as a by-law and walked it down the street and back, and at the end of it Mr Gammage was asleep, sitting up against the wall, properly, for the first time since Tuesday.</p>

## Sump

Sump was cursed in 1794, when the wasps got into the well and the Water Committee was wound up, and in 1795 the parish drafted Standing Orders for the Lifting of the Curse, six clauses, and filed them, and never used them, because a curse that has been properly drafted against is, the parish felt, more or less under control. Things used to be better. Nobody in Sump could say when, exactly, but there was a general sense of it.

Mr Gammage was born into it, in 1926, and grew up, and worked as a hurdle-maker for fifty years, and married, and was widowed, and through all of it never once, he says, had a proper night's sleep, or a proper afternoon's, or got off at all, really, after the first ten minutes. It was the curse. Everybody in Sump knew it was the curse. It was the only thing in Sump everybody agreed on.

## The riddle

In the year 2000 the parish, with a Chosen One present and Merribell of the Order of Whatever Works in attendance, followed the Standing Orders of 1795 in order. Clause five required the eldest to speak the riddle. Mr Gammage was the eldest. He opened one eye.

> "What's wet, and in the middle of the village, and we've not had any since 1794?"

He shut it again. It had, he said, taken them six generations, that one. Then the curse was called by its true name, and Merribell let a coil of rope and a tin cup down the well, which she had in her bag, of course, and it came up full, clear and cold, and only very slightly wasp-flavoured.

<div class="marginalia">I asked him, afterwards, why the parish had taken six generations over a riddle whose answer was the well. He said that the riddle had been easy; it was the asking that had been hard; you could not ask a thing like that if you had not slept since Tuesday, and nobody in Sump had slept since Tuesday since 1794. Then he shut his eyes. I waited. He was asleep. - H.B.</div>

## Abel Thrush

Sump was the far end of Abel Thrush's road, and Mr Gammage told Piper Quill, for her elegy, that for forty years, every Tuesday, a bottle of Fenmarch water was left on their well, by Abel, and nobody drank it, because it was not Sump water, and Sump did not take charity. She put that in the elegy: *He brought the water nobody would drink.* Mr Gammage says that it is the best line in it, and that he drinks Fenmarch water now, out of respect, every Tuesday, and that it is very good water, and that they were fools.

## Afterwards

He is seventy-six. He sleeps. He sleeps after lunch, and after tea, and in church, and once, in 2001, during the parish council's motion to put up a plaque about the lifting of the curse, which was carried without him. He says that he is catching up, and that he has two hundred and six years to catch up on, on behalf of the parish, and that he is doing his best. The plaque reads: SUMP. CURSED 1794. NOT, 2000. QUIET PLEASE. MR GAMMAGE IS ASLEEP.
