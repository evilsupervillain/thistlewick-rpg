<p class="lede"><b>Prewitt</b> is the Sub-Librarian of <a href="../Institutions/The_Collegium_Arcanum">the Collegium Arcanum</a>, and sits at a desk just inside the Library door with nothing on it but an hourglass and a very large ledger, in a navy jacket and a tie. She is immovable, courteous and correct, and those are the three words everybody uses, and I have tried for a fourth and not found one. She has never refused anybody anything. She has also, as far as I can discover, never once let anybody borrow a book.</p>

## Not from this desk

She greets everybody the same way, and means it:

> "Good afternoon. Prewitt, Sub-Librarian. How may the Library help you?"

A book may be borrowed on a reader's ticket. The Library issues reader's tickets on a letter from a Fellow. The Fellows do not come out. The only letter most visitors can get is Mr Gedge's card from the Porter's Lodge, which he writes, countersigns and issues in four seconds, and which she will not accept, very kindly, on the grounds that Mr Gedge is not a Fellow: "Mr Gedge is a great deal more than a Fellow, and the Library cannot accept his letters on that account." I have thought about this sentence a good deal since, and I believe every word of it is true, and I believe Mr Gedge would say so too.

It is not a no. She was quite firm about that.

> "Oh, it is never a no. Nothing here is ever a no. It is 'not from this desk'."

She can refer you to the Committee for Readers instead, which meets in the Library, so that its members must all hold a reader's ticket. She says this kindly, and correctly, and she does not move at all, and if you come back she will tell you that a referral is very nearly the same as a ticket, and takes longer. There is a young man at the reading table who asked for a book at Michaelmas. It has been referred to the shelf it is on.

She was born in 1946, and came to the Library desk in 1971, as Sub-Librarian, which she still is, because there is no Librarian above her for her to succeed. The post of Librarian has been with a committee since before she came. I asked her whether she minded. She said the Library had not asked her to mind, and that she would not like to do it without a referral.

I also asked what the hourglass is for. She said that it measures an hour.

## The Register

The Register of Outstanding Fines lies on her desk, on a pile of lesser registers. It is the size of a gravestone, and has a brass clasp, which is locked, and it is confidential, and very heavy, and Mr Gedge lifts it for her on Mondays. Most of what is in it was, in the year 2000, in one account. She could not say whose. It was the largest number she had ever written down, and she wrote it down again every morning, with the day's interest, for eleven years, and it was never once wrong.

I have thought about that too. Every morning for eleven years she came in, and unlocked the clasp, and took yesterday's largest number in the world and made it larger, by hand, and checked it, and it was right. It was not her number. It was not owed to her. She did it because it was correct, and the Library does not leave a correct thing undone merely because nobody will ever pay it.

In the year 2000 somebody came about it, and Mr Gedge was sent for to lift the Register, although it was not a Monday, which I am told has not happened before or since. What happened at the desk that afternoon is in the article on <a href="Zephyrine_Vance">Miss Vance</a>, so far as anything is, and I am not going to add to it from here. What I can say, because it is true either way, is that the Sub-Librarian ruled a line under the largest number she had ever written down, and wrote something under the line, in capitals, and that she looks at it sometimes, the way you might look at a view.

## The binding

Later the same year <a href="Wren_Halloway">Wren Halloway</a> brought the Library a manuscript, *<a href="../Bestiary/The_Fauna_of_the_Valley">The Fauna of the Valley, by What They Are</a>*, with eleven type specimens, three reviewers (one of them the duck) and a list of subscribers, which had been left on the Library desk with the Library's pen. Miss Prewitt bound it herself, by hand. She had not bound anything for anybody in eleven years. Then she took it to the shelf of the Collegium's monographs and it went into the gap there, which was exactly an inch wide.

I asked her who the last one had been, eleven years before. She said that the Library does not discuss its readers.

<div class="marginalia">I asked the Sub-Librarian, for this history, whether I might borrow the Collegium's own copy of its statutes, to check a clause. She was very sorry. I had no letter from a Fellow. She referred me to the Committee for Readers, and my name has been on its list since the spring of 2001, and the Committee, which meets in the Library, has written twice to say that it would be glad to see me at its next meeting, on production of a reader's ticket. I have checked the clause another way. She asked to be told that I had, so that she could close the referral, and I told her, and she did. - H.B.</div>

## Afterwards

She is at the desk. The Register is a little lighter than it was; only a little. Mr Pennock, the Registrar's clerk, sits at the east end of the same desk now, with one file on top of his pile, and they get on, I am told, in the way two people get on who have both been correct about the same thing for some time.

She has not bound anything since. Asked whether she would, she said that it would depend on the book, and that the Library would be glad to consider it. The young man at the reading table is still waiting, and his referral has been referred, and she brings him a glass of water at four, which is not in the statutes, and which nobody has asked her to stop.
