<p class="lede"><b>The courier</b> is a man in a red coat and a flat cap, with a satchel on a strap, who carries the post. Wherever the Forty-Eighth's company went in the year 2000, he was already there, standing about as if he had been there all morning. They left him in one town and found him in the next. They never passed him on the road, and there was only the one road. He never explained, and after a while nobody asked him.</p>

## Post?

He says very little. To anybody with a letter to send he says, "Post?" When he has it he says where it is going and when it will get there: "Upper Clanging. Dinner hour." Asked what he is doing, he says, "On my rounds," and does not say whose.

He was there, in the end, for <a href="Spare_Cotterill">Spare Cotterill</a>, who had promised his mother that he would write. Spare wrote from eight towns, four lines a time, on his knee, with his tongue out, and handed every letter to the courier, and the courier took it up the valley to <a href="../Places/Upper_Clanging">Upper Clanging</a>, where <a href="Nora_Cotterill">Mrs Cotterill</a> read it out to the shift at the works at the dinner hour. Every one got there by the dinner hour. The towns were a long way apart, and some of them were not on a road at all, and one of them was on a rock in the sky. As the letters went on the party's notes on where they found him got shorter:

> A man in a red coat and a flat cap, with a satchel on a strap, is standing here as if he has been here all morning.
>
> The man in the red coat. He got here first. His boots are dry.
>
> The courier is here. Nobody asks him how any more.
>
> The courier is here, eating an apple. He has nearly finished it.
>
> The courier is here. Of course he is here.

## The shortest round

At <a href="../Places/Much_Pottering">Much Pottering</a> he was there for anybody, because Effie Gotobed's letters come to Much Pottering. He had one in his hand for her with a drawing on the envelope. Spare was in front of him, and he said "Post?", and Spare looked past him at the girl by the gate and said, "Not today. She is right there." The courier nodded, as if that was often the case.

At the end of that afternoon the two of them each wrote one there and then, on their knees, and handed them to him. He took them both, walked the four steps from one to the other, and handed them over. "Much Pottering," he said. "Upper Clanging." It was the shortest round he has ever done, and he did it properly.

## On the Stair

The last time the company saw him was on <a href="../Places/The_Stair">the Stair</a>, under a mountain that had been shut longer than anybody could remember, at the edge of their lamplight. He said "Post?" Spare had one more letter. It did not take long, and it was the first one he had sent that was entirely true. The courier said, "Upper Clanging. Dinner hour," and touched his cap to Spare, and went on down the Stair into the dark, without a lamp. Somebody asked how he had got in. Somebody else asked how he was going to see. Nobody had an answer to either.

## Afterwards

He is on his rounds. I have seen him myself, which I mention because the next thing I say will not be believed: he brings my post. Spare Cotterill's thirty-one letters came by him, and so did Mr Hollowell's statement from the Former Premises, with its second sheet. I have asked him his name, and where he is based, and how he got down off the rock. He said, "On my rounds."

<div class="marginalia">In the spring of 2001 I made a test of it. I posted a letter to myself, by him, at the end of Nether Sopping's front, and walked straight home by the short way, which takes four minutes. He was on my step. He handed it to me. He had nearly finished an apple. I have decided not to make any more tests, and I have filed the letter, unopened, under Matters Not Anticipated, a heading I have borrowed from Mr Pell. - H.B.</div>
