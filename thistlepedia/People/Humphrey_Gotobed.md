<p class="lede"><b>Humphrey Gotobed</b> is a retired adventurer of <a href="../Places/Much_Pottering">Much Pottering</a>, where nothing has ever happened, and where he has lived since 1983, when he came down the lane on a quest to rescue a lady and knocked on the wrong door. It was Marjorie Thorne's. He has been rescuing her ever since, mostly from spiders. He was out on both of the occasions that I called, and I have written this article from his things, which say a great deal, and from his wife, who says the rest.</p>

## The road

He was born in 1944 in a railway cottage at Brinkworth Halt, which is a junction on the old lines where trains stopped on the way to everywhere and nobody ever got off, and he says that he grew up watching people go past to places and decided at an early age that he would like to go to all of them. He went at eighteen, in 1962, with a sword and a pair of very good boots and a map of the world, and he set out to go down every road on it.

The map is on the wall of the Gotobeds' bedroom, over the drawers. It has a dotted red line on it, which is every road Mr Gotobed ever took, and it goes to every edge of the map, and then it comes back, quite straight, to Much Pottering. Much Pottering is circled twice, and beside it, in the same red ink, is written: FOUND IT.

<div class="marginalia">The red line goes, I noticed, nowhere near <a href="../Places/Thistlewick">Thistlewick</a>, or the <a href="../Places/The_Obligatory_Tower">Obligatory Tower</a>, or the <a href="../Places/The_Gloamwood">Gloamwood</a>. I asked Mrs Gotobed about that. She said that he had always said that there was a Chosen One for that sort of thing, and that it was rude to take another man's quest. I think this is the most considerate thing I have ever heard of an adventurer doing, and I have heard of a great many adventurers. - H.B.</div>

## The career

He was an adventurer for twenty-one years, of the kind that does not get into books: he found lost sheep and lost rings and one lost bishop, he cleared a great many cellars of rats and two of something worse, he walked a great many people to places they were frightened to walk to on their own, and he was paid, he says, generally in dinners. He killed nothing larger than a boar, which he regrets, because it was a very old boar and he thinks now it only wanted to be left alone, and he has had a soft spot for pigs ever since, which in Much Pottering, where the pig has right of way, is just as well.

## The wrong door

In the spring of 1983 he took his last quest, to rescue a lady from a tower on the far side of the county, and on the way, in the rain, he knocked on the wrong door. Marjorie Thorne opened it. He told her that he had come to rescue her. She told him that she did not need rescuing, and that he had better come in, because it was raining.

The lady in the tower, it turned out, had got herself out on the Tuesday, by the stairs, which nobody had thought to try. She sent on the reward anyway, to the adventurer who had been engaged, who was him. It is on the dresser in the Gotobeds' bedroom, on its stand, where it can be seen from the bed: a necklace, one green stone on a gold chain. Under the stand is a card in his hand, and it is the best thing in the house:

<div class="stamp">THE REWARD, FOR WHOEVER RESCUED THE LADY. SHE GOT HERSELF OUT, AND SENT IT ON TO ME. SHE WROTE: GIVE IT TO THE ONE YOU DID RESCUE. I DID.</div>

## Retired

He hung up his swords in 1984, on the bedroom wall, crossed, with a brass plate under them, H. GOTOBED - ADVENTURER (RETIRED), and he oils them every Sunday and puts them back exactly crossed. Nothing in Much Pottering has needed slaying since. He puts this down to the swords. Mrs Gotobed puts it down to Much Pottering. Both of them are right, and both of them know, and neither will say so.

Under the kitchen clock is a card in his hand: NOTHING HAPPENS AT ANY OF THESE TIMES. He put it up on the morning after the wedding, and he says it is the only promise he has ever made that he has been able to keep to the minute.

## The daughter

He taught Effie to read off the map. He is, his wife says, the reason Effie thinks there might be a volcano somewhere, because he has seen one, on the edge of the map, in 1971, and has told her so, and Effie thinks that if there was one there, there might as well be one here. He has never once told her there is not. He says that it is not an adventurer's business to tell anybody what is not at the end of a road.

## Afterwards

He is fifty-seven. He was out when I called, the first time rescuing Mrs Throssell's cat from the oak (it was the branch, which is marked, and then the trunk), and the second time walking <a href="Mr_Cobley">Mr Cobley</a> round the pond, in case Old Tom should turn over again, which he has not. I left my questions with Mrs Gotobed. They came back by Effie, in the post, a fortnight later, answered in red ink, on a map.
