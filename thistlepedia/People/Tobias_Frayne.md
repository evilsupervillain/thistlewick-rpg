<p class="lede"><b>Tobias Frayne</b> lives in a cottage on the coast road, and has been revived by passing adventurers eleven times in thirty years, on the grounds that they were going that way anyway. He was never in danger, and did not think he was. He had found a thing that always worked out, and a great many kind people, and he liked both. He has not died since 2000, on medical advice.</p>

## The cottage

It is one room, with a green roof, on a square of grass between the coast road and the beach. Visitors are welcome, and told to come in, and to mind the ladder. "Well - don't mind it. Stand wherever you like."

The ladder goes up the wall to a hatch in the ceiling, and its third rung was, until recently, cracked clean through. "Goes like a biscuit. Thirty years I've meant to mend it, and it's never quite been the moment." A new rung leant against the wall beside it, planed and sound and still in its paper, and the paper had gone yellow.

On the table there is a book, ruled in four columns, WHEN, WHAT, WHO, REMARKS, with eleven entries in it. I give seven, as he wrote them:

<div class="stamp">Ladder. A paladin. Very kind.<br>
Well. Two elves. Sang to me.<br>
Bull. A cleric. Cried a bit.<br>
Ladder. Same paladin. Less kind.<br>
Cliff. Four of them. Carried me up.<br>
Ladder. A wizard. Did it from the gate.<br>
Bull. Same cleric. Laughed this time.</div>

and four more in the same hand. Every remark in the last column is some way of saying *lovely*. There is a shelf of tonics, liniments and smelling salts, hardly any of them in his hand: each label has a date on it, and a name, and *in case*, from the eleven, who came back afterwards with something for him. And there was a stoneware jar on the table, the lid tied down with string, heavy with crowns, labelled in the book's hand: FOR WHOEVER IS GOING THAT WAY.

## The eleven

"Oh, nobody DIES on the coast road," he says. "Somebody always comes by. Adventurers. They're going that way anyway, they say. And they're ever so kind about it. Kneel down. Hold your hand. Ask your name. Ask it twice, sometimes. You don't get that from the parish."

I set this down without comment, because it does not need one, and because Mr Frayne does not think there is anything to comment on, and I was brought up to take a man's word about his own house. The first was in 1970. The book had a twelfth line, ruled, and dated each day, with nothing in it, and the pen beside it, uncapped.

## The treatment

In the year 2000 <a href="Merribell">Merribell</a> called, with the Forty-Eighth, and he offered to pop up the ladder while the kettle was on, and she said that he would not, and had his wrist before he reached it, and counted. Pulse, good. Colour, good. Collarbone, set four times. Wrist, set twice, by a paladin, beautifully. ("Didn't charge, either.")

> "There is nothing wrong with you, Mr Frayne. That's the trouble."

She looked at the book, and at the shelf of tonics with other people's names on them, and at the twelfth line. "I am not going to raise you a twelfth time. Nobody is. That is the treatment." He asked who would come by, then. She said the parish. Ask them to tea. Through the door. On purpose. He asked whether you could do that. "People do it every day, and they don't fall off anything first."

He thought about that for a long time. Then he cut the string on the jar with the bread knife and tipped it out on the table, a hundred crowns for every one of the eleven, put by for thirty years for whoever would not take it, and said that it was for whoever was going that way, and that she was going that way; she had said. She said thank you, Mr Frayne. Then she said that now they would leave, and that that was the other half of it, and on the way out she told the company not to look back, because it was part of it. Behind them the door shut, and the kettle went on, and nobody went up the ladder.

## Afterwards

He has had the parish to tea. "Twice. Nobody fell off anything." That was in 2000; it has been a good many times since, and the parish comes through the door, like people, and he says it is lovely, and means it the same way the book does. He mended the ladder himself, not very well. It holds. The new rung is fitted by somebody who has never fitted a rung before, and the old paper is in the fire.

The twelfth line in the book is still empty. The pen has been capped, and put away in the drawer. The jar is on the table, empty, with the string cut, and the label still says FOR WHOEVER IS GOING THAT WAY, and he has started, he tells me, putting crowns back into it, a few at a time, for no reason he can explain, except that somebody might be.
