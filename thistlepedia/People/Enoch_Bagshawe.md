<p class="lede"><b>Enoch Bagshawe</b> is the first bassoon of the Works Band at <a href="../Places/Upper_Clanging">Upper Clanging</a>, which is a brass band, and he is aware. In the year 2000 he had an engagement with it, the band's first, and the only road to it was through <a href="../Places/The_Gloamwood">the Gloamwood</a> by night, with eight feet of folded tube in a case and one reed. <a href="Nix">Nix</a> took him through. Nothing so much as looked at him.</p>

## The milestone

There is a milestone at the mouth of the Gloamwood, and in the year 2000, on more evenings than he will now admit to, there was somebody sitting on it in a good coat, holding a long case upright between his knees like a man holding a small tree. He introduced himself to the Forty-Eighth's party the way he introduced himself to me, all at once, as if he had been rehearsing it on the milestone, which I think he had:

> "Enoch Bagshawe. First bassoon. The Works Band, at Upper Clanging. Tonight. They've never had one."

And then, because everybody's face does the same thing at that point:

> "It's a brass band. I'm aware. They asked. Nobody asks."

He was born in 1949, on the south side of the wood, and has played the bassoon since he was eleven, in his front room, for thirty-nine years, because there is no band on the south side of the Gloamwood, and the one on the north side had never had a bassoon in it. He had played the whole of the repertoire, he told me, by himself, and very little of it had a bassoon part, so he had played the other parts. In the spring of 2000 the Works Band wrote and asked him. He has the letter. It is one line long, and it is from the bandmaster, and it says: *We have heard there is a bassoon. Could it come up?*

## The reed

The difficulty was the reed. A bassoon reed is two thin blades of cane, bound together, which a player makes by hand and nobody else can make for him, and his took three weeks, and he had one. If anything so much as looked at him in the wood he would drop the case, and that would be the reed, and that would be that. I asked whether the case was heavy. He said what he had said to the party: "Eight feet of tube. Folded." Then he went a little pink, and told me, as he had told them, that Mrs Bagshawe says he carries it more carefully than he carried her over the doorstep. She says it every anniversary. He says she is right.

<a href="Nix">Nix</a> looked at the trees for a while, and then explained the knack. She does not stop encounters. She halves them. He asked what half an encounter was. "Still an encounter," she said. So it was to be none at all, and he recommended mothballs, which the Band keeps its uniforms in, and nothing has come near the Band in years. Any shop has them; fifty steps a twist, and then you want another, but not straight off, because they want airing. "Halved and mothballed," Nix said. "Belt and braces."

## The note

They went in at dark, the whole length of the Gloamwood, by night, with the case held to his chest and the wood looking, he says, as if it had been rude to him. It did not look at him once. At the north end the trees stopped, and there was a road, and a moon on it, and far up the valley the lights of the works town, and Mr Bagshawe stood in the road and looked back at the wood. "Nothing. Not one thing. Did you see anything?" Nix said that she had not seen a thing, and that that was the knack.

Then he opened the case, there in the road, and checked the reed by moonlight, and played one long low note on it, for no reason but that he could. People in Upper Clanging tell me they heard it, three miles off, and thought it was the works. He paid the fee, three hundred, in the envelope, exact, and Nix asked for a receipt, and he wrote one on his knee, and she read it twice and filed it. Then he went off up the valley at a pace that suggested the Works Band had started without him. It had. It always had, he said. It was a brass band.

<div class="marginalia">I went to Upper Clanging to hear the Works Band for this article, and stood at the back of the Parish Rooms. Mr Bagshawe was in the second row, in a uniform which smelt very strongly of mothballs and had been let out at the shoulders for him. The band has no music written for a bassoon, and nobody has ever written any, so he plays the tuba's part, an octave up, from the tuba's copy, which he shares with the tuba. The tuba says it has made a great difference. I could not hear the bassoon at all, and said so afterwards, and he was delighted, and said that that was correct, and that I could not have heard it if it was not there either, and that there was a difference, and that the band could hear it. - H.B.</div>

## Afterwards

He is still first bassoon, and has been joined by nobody, so he is also the last. He goes up for every engagement now by daylight, the afternoon before, with a twist of mothballs in each pocket, and stays the night at the inn, and comes home the day after. He has not had to be taken through again, and has not needed to be. He still has the same reed. He has had words with it.

Mrs Bagshawe has been up twice to hear him, and says that she could not hear him either, and that she was very proud.
