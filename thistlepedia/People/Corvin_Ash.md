<p class="lede"><b>Corvin Ash</b> sat in the corner of the <a href="../Places/The_Slain_Wyvern">Slain Wyvern</a> from the age of nineteen, waiting for a destiny, for eleven years, which is longer than most careers. His village was destroyed by flooding, in his absence, and everyone was fine, and they moved eight miles inland, and there is a school now, and it is very good, and he could not make anybody understand.</p>

"...Leave," he told the Forty-Eighth. "This road ends in ash." Then he told him about the flood, and the school, and asked him not to look pleased, and went to get his coat.

I sat across the room from him for most of those eleven years, at the table by the fire, and I want to put it on record at the start that he is not nothing, and never was. He is a genuinely dangerous man with a black sword who sat still for a decade because somebody once told him he was important. The sitting still was the only part of it that was ever a waste.

## Ashcombe

He was born at <a href="../Places/Ashcombe">Ashcombe</a>, in a valley that is now a lake. In 1989, when he was nineteen and away from home, the water came up, and the village was flooded, and nobody was hurt, and the whole of it moved eight miles inland (eight miles by the road; the lake says less) and built itself again. He took the name Ash when it went under, as a memorial, after thinking about the gesture for some weeks. The village also kept the name, because it was its name and it was still using it, and so the new Ashcombe is Ashcombe too, and has a post office. You cannot mourn a place that is answering its correspondence.

The new Ashcombe is proud of the flood. It has a plaque about it, which is upbeat, and a Drainage Museum, and an annual dinner.

## The fortune

At a fair, the same year, a fortune-teller called Mother Selvidge told him, for two crowns, that he was the rival of a great hero. He organised his entire adult life around it. He went and sat in the corner of the Slain Wyvern, which is where a great hero's rival goes, and waited for the hero, and the drinks there were, he points out, somewhat cheaper.

## On the Quest

The hero came in at last, in 2000, and asked him along, and he said "If fate demands it", twice, the second time more loudly, so that it would be clear that fate had. He did dark damage, more of it the worse things went, which on the Quest was often.

**Ashcombe, new.** He went home. Everyone was delighted to see him. His mother is fine, and has a new kitchen. His childhood bedroom was rebuilt, larger, with better light, and is a study. He was invited to speak at the Flood Dinner, and did, and it went well. He gave the village its name back. His mother knitted him a scarf in the wrong colour, and he wears it.

**Ashcombe, old.** The lake is drawn down one week a year for the sluices, and if you are there that week you can walk into the old street. The party was. There is a pub sign, a doorstep, and, in a strongbox in what was the schoolroom, his own school report, which the schoolmistress had kept because she kept everybody's. It says that he will go far, if he ever gets up. On the doorstep, cut in by his grandfather in 1949, is the name of the black sword, which his grandfather made, as a doorstop: MIND THE STEP. That is its name. I am told by everybody who was there that this was the one moment in his story that was allowed to be simply sad, and that it lasted until somebody read out the report.

**The Sable Hart.** There is an inn on the moor where doomed rivals brood professionally. Every corner is taken. There is a waiting list for the darkest, kept fairly, on a rota, and a newsletter. Corvin had been a member for years and had never attended, because attending would be admitting there was a category. There is a league table. He was low on it. He is second now, behind a scar. He has a chair in the middle of the room, in the light. He attends.

**Mother Selvidge.** She is still working the fairs, and did not remember him, and read him again for two crowns, and got a long life, a useful trade, and no destiny whatsoever. He asked her to do it again, and she did, and got the same. On the third reading she put the cards down and told him, kindly and without mystery, that she says the rival thing to about four young men a year, because it is the reading that makes them stand up straight, and that it has never once been wrong, in the sense that they all went and did something. She has read him three times. She has never once been wrong.

**The appraisal.** He took the sword to <a href="Miss_Fettle">Miss Fettle</a> at the Smiths' Hall in <a href="../Places/Great_Dunnage">Great Dunnage</a>, expecting a curse. There is no curse. It is a very good sword in poor condition, and she was enthusiastic about the tang. He asked how much a curse would cost. It is not expensive. There is a form. He has the form, if he wants one.

## Afterwards

He was asked back to the Flood Dinner in 2001, and went, and was early.

He is up. He has the scarf, the chair at the Sable Hart, the form, which he has not filled in, and a long life and a useful trade still to come. He does not sit in the corner of the Slain Wyvern any more. When he comes in, which he does, he sits at a table in the middle, and on one occasion he sat at mine, and asked how the history was going, and listened to the end of the answer.

<div class="marginalia">He has asked me to remove the line about the drinks being cheaper. I have checked it, and they are, and I have left it in.</div>
