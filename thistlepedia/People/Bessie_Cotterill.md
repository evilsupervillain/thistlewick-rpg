<p class="lede"><b>Bessie Cotterill</b>, born Bessie Marsden, is the wife of Cotter Cotterill, the eldest of the Cotterills of <a href="../Places/Upper_Clanging">Upper Clanging</a>, and the granddaughter of <a href="Winnie_Marsden">Winnie Marsden</a> of the Class of 'Nineteen. She has her grandmother's face, forty years on, which everybody in the town remarks on, and which Winnie says is the face's own business. She married into a family of ten and has her dinner with them every day, because there is always more than they need.</p>

## The Marsdens

She was born in 1977, in the house behind the Safety Valve, the only child of Winnie's only son, a pattern-maker at the foundry, and was brought up in the town on the clear understanding that the Marsdens were the Class of 'Nineteen's own family and that there was a seat at the dinner with her name on it one day, near the top, as soon as somebody died, which Winnie said briskly and nobody was to take to heart.

She was christened Elizabeth, which nobody has ever used, except the vicar once, and he apologised.

## The match

She and Cotter Cotterill had known each other all their lives, in the sense that in Upper Clanging you cannot not know a Cotterill, there being too many of them, and she had walked past him at the chapel door every Sunday for twenty years. She says that he asked her out on the first Saturday he walked home from <a href="../Places/Great_Dunnage">Great Dunnage</a>, in 1994, eleven miles, and that he was too tired to say anything else, and that she said yes to stop him falling over. He says he asked on the second Saturday, and that on the first he had been too tired to ask. <a href="Nora_Cotterill">Mrs Cotterill</a> says it was the third, and that she knows, because he came in and ate two dinners.

They married in September 1999, at the chapel, on a Saturday, so that he could walk home for it. Winnie Marsden spent the reception drawing the Cotterills into the Class of 'Nineteen's seating plan, and found that there was no column for nine of them, ten in the spring, and that the plan had been settled when she was four, and she went home early and sat up with it, and nobody has been told what she said to it.

## Her dinner

She lives with Cotter in a cottage two doors up from the Cotterills', and has her dinner at the house at the end of the street every day, because Mrs Cotterill does more potatoes and there is always more than they need. "Mrs Cotterill says I am to ask her anything at all," she told the Forty-Eighth, of the baby that was coming in the summer. "My gran says a lady does not discuss it."

She has the house to herself one evening a month, on the second Saturday, when her gran has her dinner. She would like it known that she has never once told her gran how much she looks forward to it, and that she has done the arithmetic, and that if the Class of 'Nineteen had settled on a weekly dinner when they were four she would never have married at all.

## The cradle

There was a cradle by her range in the year 2000. Nobody had mentioned it, and everybody had looked at it.

It was her gran's. It had gone years ago, out of Winnie's back room, in 1989, and nobody in the family had said anything, because the Marsdens do not say things. In the year 2000 it came back. It was on the doorstep one morning, polished, with a label tied to it in a clear hand, and it is entered in a ledger in <a href="../Places/Nether_Sopping">Nether Sopping</a> in a column headed RETURNED, ruled with a ruler. "Gran's cradle?" she said, when she was asked about it. "It went years ago." And then: "Nobody has said anything."

Nobody has. Winnie came round the next day and looked at the cradle for some time and said that it wanted a new rocker, and went home. Bessie says that was the most she has ever said about anything.

## Afterwards

The baby came in the summer of 2000, as she had said it would, on time. Mrs Cotterill says it is the second Cotterill ever to be on time, after <a href="Washer_Cotterill">Washer</a>, and that it is a very good start. The baby is not named in this encyclopedia, at Winnie's request, until the seating plan is settled. The seating plan has a high chair in it now, in pencil, which Winnie drew in herself. It is the first thing on that plan that has ever been in pencil, and Bessie says it is the first time she has ever known her gran to leave anything open to revision.

She still has her dinner at the end of the street. She still has the house to herself on the second Saturday, though now it is the house, and Cotter, and the baby, and she says that is a better house.
