<p class="lede"><b>Lettice Moon</b> of <a href="../Places/Great_Dunnage">Great Dunnage</a> is a seamstress, and the author and only performer of a ballad, Sad, in fourteen verses, in which everybody dies, including the narrator, who comes back for the chorus. She entered it in the Contest at the Sign of the Bell in the year 2000, and several of the back table were visibly moved, and said so, loudly.</p>

## The ballad

It has no title. She says that a title would give away the ending, and that the ending is the point. It begins with a shepherd and a miller's daughter and a wedding fixed for the spring, and I have been asked not to say what happens after the second verse, but I can say that by the eleventh there is nobody left in the parish, and that the twelfth is about the dog. In the thirteenth the narrator dies, of grief, mid-line. In the fourteenth the narrator comes back for the chorus, because, as she explains, somebody has to sing it.

She wrote the first verse at fourteen, at her sewing, and has added to it ever since, a verse every year or so, as things occurred to her. She sings at her work. Her employer, Mrs Aske, of Cordwainers' Row, has asked her to keep it to the chorus during fittings, because of the effect on the customers, and she does, mostly.

## The Contest

> "Moon. Ballad (Sad). Fourteen verses. Nobody survives."

She went second, after Mr Dray. Nine from Mr Ambler, two from Miss Quayle, five from Mrs Harbottle, who said afterwards that she had marked the craft and not the body count. Sixteen. When Piper Quill took the stage with a Ballad (Not Sad), which nobody had ever entered, Miss Moon was part of the floor, with Mr Dray and the back table, and contributed, as is the custom of the Bell, a very sad verse of her own from the side, to bring the room's spirits down to where she felt they ought to be. Somewhere in the fourth verse she sat down, to a smattering, and heard the rest.

It was a ballad about a farm boy who walked north, and nobody died in it, and it was all true. She told me that she sat through the whole of it waiting for somebody to die, and that nobody did, and that she found she did not mind. The next time the Forty-Eighth's company saw her, she said:

> "I've changed the ending. Somebody survives verse three. Briefly."

## Afterwards

She is twenty-six. The ballad is now fifteen verses, because the survivor of verse three needed a verse of his own to die in. She is working on a sixteenth, in which he might not. She has not decided. She has written to Miss Quill, care of the Wyvern, to ask how it is done, and Miss Quill has written back, in the third person, four pages, and Miss Moon says that she has read them several times and is not sure she agrees with any of it, and is trying it anyway. She placed second in the Contest of 2001. Mrs Harbottle gave her a six. It was for the new verse.

<div class="marginalia">She sang me the whole ballad, fifteen verses, in the back room of the shop, between two fittings, so that I would get the article right. I have got it as right as I can without telling you what happens to the dog. I will say that I had not expected to mind about the dog. - H.B.</div>
