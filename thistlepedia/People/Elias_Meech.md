<p class="lede"><b>Elias Meech</b> farms the top field in the snowy north where, in 1749, on a Tuesday, a round grey stone fell out of the sky, which his great-great-grandfather set up for a gatepost, because it was there. It was the best gatepost in the county. It outlasted eleven gates. In the year 2000 he was between gates, and traded it to Hob Grumnir for one made of all eleven, and it is a sword now, and he leans on the gate.</p>

## The top field

He was born in 1931 in the room over the dairy, the only son of the fourth Meech since the stone, and has farmed the top field since 1955. He says that in all that time the stone was the least trouble of anything on the farm: it never needed feeding, mending, shearing or worrying about, and it held a gate, and pilgrims came up every summer to look at it, and kept the frost off the shrine.

> "That one. Came down out of the sky in 1749, into the top field, on a Tuesday. My great-great-grandad set it up for a gatepost, because it was there. Best gatepost in the county. It's outlasted eleven gates."

He has never thrown a gate out. All eleven are in the hedge-bottom along the top field, where each was put when it went, rusting quietly in order. He says that you never know, and he was right.

## The pilgrims

> "That's the pilgrims. They call it the Sky Anvil. Built that round the foot of it in 1750, and they come up every summer to look at it. I don't mind. Keeps the frost off."

He gives them tea. He says that he has given tea to pilgrims every summer since 1955, about forty at a time, and that not one of them has ever asked what the stone was, because they knew, and that they have always been very well behaved, and only ever wanted to stand and look at it, which he did not understand, and does now.

## The trade

The last gate went at Michaelmas 1999. He was between gates. He would not sell the stone; it was not for sale; it was a very good gatepost. "I'd take a gate for it, mind." Asked what he would hang a gate on, then, he said the shrine, which was a very good shrine. "It is. Wants a gate, mind."

In the year 2000 Hob Grumnir came up with the Forty-Eighth's company, and put his hand on the stone, and said it was star-iron. "On a Tuesday," said Mr Meech. Hob asked what a gate could be made out of, up here, and Mr Meech said: "Out of what? I've no iron. Never thrown a gate out, mind." Hob had the eleven out of the hedge-bottom, and worked the afternoon through, cold, and made a gate out of eleven gates, true on its hangings, with a spring on the latch he had carried since a churchyard in Thistlewick. Mr Meech hung it on the shrine himself.

> "...That's a very good gate. Go on, then. Take it. Mind your back."

> "Pilgrims'll miss it."

<div class="marginalia">They did not. That summer the pilgrims came up as usual, and found the stone gone, and a gate on the shrine, and stood and looked at it, and then one of them opened it, and shut it, and then they all did, all afternoon. "Best gate in the county," Mr Meech says. "Pilgrims come up and open it and shut it all afternoon. Nobody's ever given them anything to open before." - H.B.</div>

## Afterwards

He is seventy-one. He leans on the gate. He has asked me to say that he has no idea who the sword is for, and that he has not asked, and that a man who trades a gatepost for a gate has had a fair bargain whatever comes of the gatepost after, and that if it is for who he thinks it is for, he would be very glad, and that he will not say who he thinks it is for either, because he has been asked not to, by a very large man with a hammer.
