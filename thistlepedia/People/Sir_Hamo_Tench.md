<p class="lede"><b>Sir Hamo Tench</b> of the <a href="../Institutions/The_Order_of_the_Errant">Order of the Errant</a> came to <a href="../Places/Weeping_Bottom">Weeping Bottom</a> for the sea air in 1986, with a cough, and has been in the bed that is exactly on the line down the middle of the ward ever since, where both halves of the Order treat him, and the cough is exactly the same. He writes to Coldbarrow every quarter, IN THE FIELD, and it is true; they have a pin in him. In the year 2000 he kept the score of the queue from his bed, and stood sponsor at Sir Aldric's vigil, and said that it was very fair.</p>

## Errant

He was born in 1921 at Coldbarrow, in the snow, the son of the Order's armourer, and went into the Order at eighteen, and kept his vigil at nineteen, and it took him four goes. He would like it known that all four were in the right chapel. He is the only knight of his year who will say how many goes it took, and he says it because he thinks it is good for the young ones to know.

He went out errant in 1941 and stayed out for forty-five years: through the north, mostly, where the Order is thin, and he says that in forty-five years he never once found the thing he was sent to find, and that he found a great many other things instead, and that the Order has never minded, because what the Order is for is not finding things. It is looking round.

## The line

In 1986 he came south with a cough, to Weeping Bottom, for the sea air, on the advice of every physician he consulted, none of whom had been. The Sisters gave him the bed at the end of the ward. When <a href="Sister_Bewick">Sister Bewick</a>'s party took the east half in 1994 and the line was painted, it went exactly through his bed, and he declined to be moved, on the grounds that a knight does not leave his post, and that the bed was his post, and that he was not going to pick a side in an argument about soup.

Both lots treat him. Soup at noon, correct treatment at four. The cough is exactly the same. Very fair.

## The vigil

In the year 2000 Sir Aldric Pemberton-Gore the Third came to Weeping Bottom with the Forty-Eighth's company.

> "Pemberton-Gore. Were you ever confirmed? The vigil, and so on. The Order's chapel for the west is across the yard. Always has been. The Sisters lend it us. There's a shield over the door, inside. Thirty years it's hung there. Nobody's come for it."

It was Sir Aldric's. He had been due to keep his vigil under it at sixteen, and kept it in the wrong chapel instead, eleven times. Sir Hamo stood sponsor at the twelfth, dusk to dawn, in his nightshirt with his surcoat over it, and the company kept the watch, and something came in under the door at midnight, and Sir Aldric's article tells the rest.

> "Sir Aldric Pemberton-Gore. Vigil kept, in the right chapel, dusk to dawn. Confirmed. Thirty years late. The Order's never minded late. It minds wrong chapels."

He told Sir Aldric what the knight on his arms was doing, looking back over his shoulder. "He's looking back to see who's behind him. That's the whole of the Order. You don't lead. You look round, and you see who's bleeding, and you go and stand in front of them." And then: "You were facing your friends."

<div class="marginalia">I asked Sir Hamo whether he had ever been confirmed in a chapel with a man who had tried eleven wrong ones. He said that it took him four goes, and that a man who had been going round the country for thirty years looking for the right chapel was a man who meant it, and that the Order would take one of those over a hundred who got it right the first time. Then he coughed, and said that it was exactly the same, and that it was very fair. - H.B.</div>

## Afterwards

He is eighty. He is still on the line, and still writes IN THE FIELD, and it is still true. Since 2000, when the line stopped meaning anything, the Sisters have offered him a bed by the window, with a view of the sea. He has declined. He says that the line is still there, painted, whether it means anything or not, and that somebody had better stand on it, in case, and that he is in the field.
