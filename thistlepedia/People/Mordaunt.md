<p class="lede"><b>Mordaunt</b> is the clerk of the Order of the Errant at its Chapter House at <a href="../Places/Coldbarrow">Coldbarrow</a>, in the snow of the far north, where he keeps the great map of the island with a pin in it for every knight in the field, and reads it the way other people read a will. One knight, one pin. That is the system. For nine years one knight had nine pins, and three of them were the same week, and in the year 2000 Mordaunt solved it, on a Thursday, by creating a rank.</p>

## The gatehouse

He was born in 1936 in the gatehouse of the Chapter House, the son of the Order's porter, and grew up under the great map, which he says was the first thing he ever learned to read, before letters, by the pins: red for knights errant, blue for knights at home, black for knights who would not be coming home, with a little card. He was made under-clerk at eighteen, in 1954, and clerk in 1968. He has never been a knight, and has never wanted to be one. He says that somebody has to know where they all are, and that a knight never does.

> "Chapter House of the Order of the Errant. Mordaunt, clerk. Are you a knight? Then you are not on the map. Good. One knight, one pin. That is the system."

## Nine pins

Sir Aldric Pemberton-Gore the Third went out errant in 1991. By the year 2000 the great map had nine red pins in it for him.

> "Sir Aldric Pemberton-Gore. The Third. Nine pins. Six of them are correct, on different dates. Three of them are the same week."

> "Frostwick, sir. And Fenmarch. And a lay-by in the west."

People said that the Order had lost his file. "The Order has not lost his file. The Order has nine of them." They are on the bottom shelf, nine fat volumes, each labelled PEMBERTON-GORE, and each certain that it is the real one. Mordaunt's ledger lay open at P, and every entry on the page had been crossed out and written in again somewhere else.

> "If you should see Sir Aldric, note the place, the date and the time. In ink. Not pencil. Pencil is how we got here."

## The committee

A committee was formed to review the map, the pins and the week. It sat on Thursdays, at a table with two chairs and two stools, and the committee was Mordaunt, appointed to it four times, and therefore quorate. In the year 2000 Sir Aldric came to Coldbarrow, and Mordaunt said that it was Thursday, and the committee would see him now, and sat in each chair and on each stool in turn, and then stood behind all of them, and gave the committee's findings.

> "Finding one: nine pins. Finding two: all nine are correct. Finding three: three of them are the same week. The committee has been unable to fault the pins. It has therefore had to fault the knight."

The committee recommended that Sir Aldric remain in one place for the duration of the review, and offered him, on that condition, Knight Commander of the Chapter: a desk, here, and a pin that does not move. It was, Sir Aldric said, the finest thing he had been offered in nine years, and he thanked the committee, and said no. A knight who stays put is a post. But Mordaunt had asked, and nobody asks a man to stay, and he would like that put in the minutes. In ink.

Mordaunt wrote for some time, and crossed nothing out.

> "Then the committee has a data problem, sir. The knight cannot stay with the pin. Resolved: that the pin go with the knight. Knight Errant, At Large. A rank of the Order, created this Thursday. And wherever the knight is, sir, the map is correct. That is the system now."

<div class="marginalia">I have seen the card on the committee's table: REVIEW OF PINS (PEMBERTON-GORE): CONCLUDED. SIR A. INFORMED. OFFERED THE CHAPTER. DECLINED. And under it, smaller, in ink: *Beautifully.* (Minuted at his request.) It is the only adverb in the minutes of the Order of the Errant since 1890. I asked Mordaunt why he had allowed it. He said that it was accurate. - H.B.</div>

## Afterwards

He is sixty-six. There are no red pins on the great map now, and in the margin a small card in his hand: PEMBERTON-GORE. AT LARGE. PIN CARRIED. Every week since 2000 he has had a note from Sir Aldric's squire, the Fourth, giving his uncle's place, the date and the time, in ink, and he keeps them in a tenth volume, and it is the only one of the ten that agrees with itself. He says that he has never been so well informed about a knight's whereabouts in fifty years at the desk, and that he has never once known where Sir Aldric was going to be next, and that he has come to regard that as the correct state of affairs for a knight errant, and that the system has never worked better.
