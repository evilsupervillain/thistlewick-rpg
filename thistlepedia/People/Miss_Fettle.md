<p class="lede"><b>Miss Fettle</b> is the Appraiser to <a href="../Institutions/The_Worshipful_Company_of_Smiths">the Worshipful Company of Smiths</a> at <a href="../Places/Great_Dunnage">Great Dunnage</a>, and sits at a bench in the Smiths' Hall with a sword on it, looking at the sword the way other people look at a sunset. She will value anything you bring her. She will also, before she has said good morning, ask after the tang, which is the part of a sword that goes up inside the handle, where nobody can see it. Everybody looks at the blade. She has made it her business not to.</p>

## The tang

She introduces herself in the middle of a sentence about something else, which is the sword:

> "Look at that. No, LOOK at it. Fettle. Appraiser to the Company. Do you see the tang?"

You cannot see the tang. It is inside the handle. I said so, as everybody says so, and she said "Exactly!", as I gather she says to everybody, and explained that it is the most important part of the whole sword, and that it is hidden. A blade without a good tang is a blade that comes off in your hand at the worst moment, and the worst moment, she says, is the only moment anybody ever really uses a sword. Everybody looks at the blade. Nobody ever asks after the tang. She always asks after the tang. Then she took her spectacles off and wiped them, because she was overcome, and put them back on, and told me that I might bring her anything I wanted valued, weapons, plate or heirlooms, and that she did not charge for tangs.

She was born in Great Dunnage in 1975, over a cutler's, and says that she was taking handles off things before she could reach the bench, and that her father let her, provided she put them back on. She was made the Company's Appraiser in 1997, at twenty-two, which the Prime Warden says was young, and was done under the Schedule, and was not regretted. Her offer to anybody who comes in with anything is the same, and I have heard it four times: she will have the handle off it in a trice. She will. I have watched.

## The black sword

In the year 2000 the Forty-Eighth's company came into the Hall with <a href="Corvin_Ash">Corvin Ash</a> in it, and her eyes went straight past all of them to the black sword at his side, and stayed there. "Oh. Oh, may I - just the handle -" He said no. She said, "Quite right. Quite right. Another time," and gave him a card, which he put away without reading, and then, when he thought nobody was looking, read.

Another time came. He unbuckled the sword and laid it on her bench, the way a man lays down a confession, and asked her to tell him what was on it. She had the handle off in a trice, while he looked at the ceiling, and then:

> "Oh, look at that. Oh, the TANG. Somebody loved this, once."

She was some minutes with the tang. He asked her about the curse. There was no curse. There was not a trace of one. It was a very good sword in very poor condition, she said, and he had been sitting in corners with it; and there was a name on the tang, in letters nobody had used for a while, which she would not like to try aloud. He was quiet for a long time, and then asked, carefully, as though it might be too good for him, how much a curse would cost, to have one put on.

> "Oh, not much. They're mostly paperwork. There's a form."

It is FORM C-1: APPLICATION FOR A CURSE (ELECTIVE), and it is in triplicate, and she keeps it in a drawer. The curse is thirty crowns and the form is free. When one is wanted she stamps the form, reads a short paragraph off the back of it in a bored voice, and blows out a candle, and that is that: it is the economy one, and it lasts until you next sleep in a proper bed. She will take it off again for nothing, by relighting the candle, and it comes off with no fuss at all. I am told that this is the only part of the service anybody has ever found disappointing.

She was right, as it turned out, about the tang. Somebody had loved it. It was his grandfather, who made the sword, and cut the same letters into a doorstep at Ashcombe, where it stood for forty years holding the door open, and I have put the rest of that in <a href="Corvin_Ash">his own article</a>, where it belongs.

## Beatrice

When <a href="Hob_Grumnir">Hob Grumnir</a> laid his hammer on the Company's table for the Assize, and Warden Crabbe had read out clauses one and two, Miss Fettle came from her bench without being asked, and turned Beatrice over in her hands for a long time, and said one thing: "Who made this?" She was, I am told by four people who were there, the only person in the Hall who thought to ask it, and she asked it first, before anybody had looked at the face. Hob said, "Not me. I'd never have got the balance." It was the answer that disqualified Beatrice under clause one, and it was Miss Fettle's question that got it out of him, and he has thanked her for it, which I do not think anybody has ever done before for a disqualification.

<div class="marginalia">I went to the Smiths' Hall to ask Miss Fettle for this article, and before I had finished asking, she asked whether she might see my pen. I gave it her. She had the cap off it in a trice, and held the nib up to the window for some time, and said, "Oh, look at that. Nobody ever asks after the nib." I said that I asked after it every morning, and that it is the only part of the history that has never once let me down, and she wiped her spectacles. She did not charge. - H.B.</div>

## Afterwards

Pinned over her bench there is now a Form C-1, stamped, and beside it a drawing of a tang, very lovingly done. Mr Ash has the form, and has not filled it in, and both of these are true, because the form is in triplicate: the top copy goes with the applicant, the second goes on file, and the third is stamped by the Appraiser on the day it is issued whatever becomes of the other two, and is the one she has pinned up. It has nothing written on it. She says that the stamp is the important part, and that it is hidden, under the drawing, and that nobody ever asks after it.

She has issued two more Forms C-1 since, both to members, in hoods, of an inn on the moor, and stamped neither, because neither of them brought thirty crowns, only the form, which is free. She gave each of them a card. The children of Great Dunnage bring her butter knives now, to be asked after, and she asks after every one of them, and puts the handles back on.

Her card says: FETTLE. Appraiser to the Company. Weapons, plate, heirlooms. Tangs: no charge. That is the whole of it. I asked her for a first name for this article, and she said that the Company's roll had never needed one, and asked whether I had seen the tang on the sword she had in that morning, and I had not, and that was the end of the interview.
