<p class="lede"><b>Nix</b> runs the back room of the general store in <a href="../Places/Thistlewick">Thistlewick</a>. She does not steal things. She <b>locates</b> them, ahead of schedule, on behalf of a client who has not yet been identified. "Everyone asks eventually," she told the Forty-Eighth, before he had asked. "I like to get ahead of it. Same as everything else."</p>

She is not a thief, and the distinction is not a joke to her, and I have come round to her view of it. Every single thing in the back room was got somehow, and she can tell you how, from the ledger, by date, and the telling is always in order and is sometimes magnificent.

## The ledger

At fifteen, in 1989, Nix worked out that "I wanted it" was not a sentence that got you anywhere in a village where everybody knows your mother, and opened a ledger instead. Every item in, every item out, every provenance, dated, in a hand that improves visibly from page to page. All of it was done on behalf of a client described in the front of the book as *the party of the second part*.

I have been allowed to read the ledger, at the counter, with her watching. It is the most honest document in the county. The Committee's minutes are longer and the Prophecy is older, and neither of them can account for a single teaspoon.

## On the Quest

She joined because the Tower was "a tower nobody's been inside for a hundred years, full of things nobody's counted". She had her coat on before he finished the sentence. Her terms were that three is a good number: "Small, quiet, splits four ways."

**The tower door.** The door of the Obligatory Tower was barred. There is a key under the mat. She did not use it. "Bar's on the inside. Classic."

**Returned.** In the front of the ledger, where the hand is worst, were eleven things she had never sold on: the longest loans she had. Bram's mother's pie dish, the Old Man's chair, the gate key, Hulda Bole's good boots, a cradle for Bessie Marsden at Upper Clanging, and six more, across three towns. "Everything's a loan, eventually. That's what the RETURNED column is for." The column had been ruled, very neatly, down eleven years of pages, and there was nothing in it. She returned all eleven in an afternoon that was not one afternoon. None of the owners had known the things were gone, every one of them was pleased to have them back, and two of them tried to pay. The ledger has a RETURNED column now, with eleven lines in it, and a double line under the last, blotted.

**The client.** On the clean page after it, the first new entry was a parcel, brown paper, which Mrs Bole had been carrying for somebody since 1989, marked NOT TO BE OPENED UNTIL I AM TWENTY-SIX. The hand on it was Nix's own, at fifteen. Inside was a letter from the party of the second part, signed *N., aged fifteen*. The client, it said, wanted one thing, which was the first entry in the book, the only line in eleven years with no provenance, which was in bond at <a class="new" href="../Places/Great_Dunnage">Great Dunnage</a>, and which she had been avoiding. "Stop it."

It was her gran's coat. It had gone down to her ankles. She would not wear it until it fitted, and would not sell it, so at fifteen she put it into the Bonded Warehouse, stood on a crate, and made a speech to the wharfinger, Mr Tibbs: *I shall return for it when I am somebody.* He wrote it in the margin. The rent came by post every quarter for eleven years, never late, no name, and she never went back, because he would have remembered the speech. He had. He unlocked the wardrobe. She put it on, and it fits. The first line of the ledger has a provenance now.

**Professional courtesy.** <a class="new" href="Cobb_Lorrimer">Cobb Lorrimer</a> steals things, says so, and wears a hood about it, and finds her whole vocabulary an insult. He challenged her to a contest: a list, an afternoon, and a client. She won on paperwork, having got everything on the list with permission. He has since taken a position in Nether Sopping, afternoons, signed for, in which he plays the Dark Lord for the children, and loses.

**The Counting House.** Great Dunnage sealed its counting house in 1788, when the key was lost, and the Corporation wanted it opened by a licensed person, on Form L. She opened it in nine seconds while the Mayor was still reading the preamble. It held one chair and a note: WE SPENT IT. Form L remains blank.

**Half an encounter.** She has a way of moving through a wood at night that halves the number of things that notice you. In 2000 the Works Band at Upper Clanging needed Mr Enoch Bagshawe, and his bassoon, brought through the Gloamwood by night without a single fight. He was brought through without one, or half of one. The Works Band has a bassoon.

## Afterwards

The back room is open. The ledger is into its second volume, which began on the page after the coat, and its first entries are already in a better hand than the last of the old one. The party of the second part has been identified, and the book is still kept on her behalf. I asked Nix whether that made any difference. She said it made the paperwork simpler, and that she had always liked to be ahead of things, and that she had now been beaten to it once, by a girl of fifteen, and did not intend it to happen again.

<div class="marginalia">I have checked my pockets after every interview. Nothing has ever been missing. On one occasion there was something extra, a pencil, rather better than mine, with a note on it in a good hand: <i>located. on behalf of the party of the second part.</i></div>
