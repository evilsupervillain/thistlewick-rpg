<p class="lede"><b>Walt Ashbury</b> sits on the bottom step of the stairs at the Eight Hours in <a href="../Places/Restwell">Restwell</a>, with a fiddle, where he can see the whole room, and plays every guest up to bed: eight bars, the same eight bars, every time, since 1974. Every guest comes down in the morning better than they went up, whatever was wrong with them, and nobody can say why, least of all the retired doctor by the fire, who has a theory, and does not like it.</p>

## The step

He was born in 1950 upstairs at the Eight Hours, in room six, on the shortest night of the year, which his mother said was the only night she ever spent there that she did not sleep through. His father kept the inn, and played the fiddle in the evenings, badly, in the bar. Walt took it up at nine. By twenty he could play anything, and did, in the bar, and the guests went up to bed at all hours and came down as tired as anybody does at an inn.

In 1974, at twenty-four, on an evening when the bar was full and he had played everything, he sat down on the bottom step of the stairs to rest, and began to play, without thinking, eight bars he had never played before, and the guest who was going up at that moment, a carter with a bad back, went up, and slept, and came down in the morning without a bad back, and said that he had not slept like that since he was a boy. Walt played the same eight bars for the next guest, and the next. He has played them for every guest since. He does not know where they came from. He says that he did not make them up, exactly; that they were there, on the step, and he played them.

> "Everybody. Twenty-six years. Same eight bars, every time."

> "It has to be that one. I tried a different one, once. They came down tired."

## The tune

I have heard it, twice: once going up, and once from the bar, sitting where I could see. It is eight bars long, in what I believe <a href="Piper_Quill">Piper Quill</a> would call a minor key that is not sad, and it goes up, and it does not come down again at the end, but stops, at the top, as if it had arrived somewhere. Miss Quill, who has stayed at the Eight Hours, has tried to write it down, and says that she can write down the notes and that the notes are not it, and that she has stopped trying, and that it is the only tune in the world she has ever stopped trying to write down.

<div class="marginalia">I went up to bed at the Eight Hours on my way back from the coast road, and he played me up, and I came down, and it was morning, and I agreed that it was. I looked at the clock and I would rather not say. I have written in the register: eight hours. Remarks: I am a historian, and I should like to have stayed longer. I have said that elsewhere in this volume. I find that I wanted to say it twice. - H.B.</div>

## The tired

The night he tried a different tune was in 1983, for a wedding party, who asked for something cheerful. He played them up with a jig. They came down in the morning tired, every one of them, and cross, and the bride said that she had not slept a wink and that she was to be married that afternoon and would look a fright, and she did, she says, in every picture, and she has never let him forget it, and she comes back every year, on the anniversary, with her husband, and asks for the eight bars, and sleeps like a top, and is delighted.

## Hester

His wife Hester has listened to those eight bars twelve hundred times a year for twenty-eight years, from the kitchen, and has never once asked him to stop. "He is very good at it." That is all she says about it. It is all he needs. He says that he would play the eight bars for nobody but the guests, and that he plays something else for her, in the kitchen, when the house is asleep, and that it is not eight bars, and that it is none of my business, and I have not asked.

## Afterwards

He is fifty-one. He is still on the step. The Eight Hours has had more guests since 2000 than ever, because the road to the <a href="../Places/The_Obligatory_Tower">Tower</a> is busy with people going to see the chair, and every one of them goes up to the eight bars and comes down better, and many of them come back on the way home. Dr Pike still watches him. Walt says that the doctor is welcome to watch, and that he has watched himself, often, and has never found out anything either.
