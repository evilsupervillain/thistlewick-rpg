<p class="lede"><b>Sir Dunstable of the Vale</b> is a knight of excellent manners who comes in through the porch of the Chapel of Continuance at <a href="../Places/Hithermost">Hithermost</a> at a quarter past four, every day, signs the Book, just in case, finds a cup of tea waiting for him on the lectern, and is delighted. He then goes through the Door into the Mountain. Nothing happens. He will tell you so, with some warmth.</p>

## The knight

Of his early life I can find almost nothing, and I have looked. He is of the Vale; he does not say which. He is a knight of the Errant, the order that keeps its great map at <a href="../Places/Coldbarrow">Coldbarrow</a>, with a pin for every knight in the field, and his pin is in the Chapel of Continuance. Mordaunt, who keeps the map, says that it is the one pin he has never had to move, and that it is right, and he says it in the tone of a man who has had to move a great many.

He holds one share of the Kingdom of <a href="../Places/Fairholme">Fairholme</a>, which can only mean that at some time he rescued the Princess Rosamund, and took the half, as everybody does, and he has a chair at the council table and a place card: SIR DUNSTABLE OF THE VALE. ONE SHARE. BACK DIRECTLY. - D. The chair has been empty at every meeting that anybody remembers, because the council meets all day, and at a quarter past four he has an appointment. The minutes give his apologies, every time, *in a mountain*. Rosamund does not say which rescue was his. She speaks of all eleven of her rescuers very kindly, and I did not like to ask.

He is a big, cheerful man, and his hand, in chalk, is the most cheerful hand in this encyclopedia.

## A quarter past four

> "Dunstable. Sir. Of the Vale. Just signed the Book. Just in case."

Asked whether he has been through before: "Oh, once or twice. Nothing happened, mind. Nothing at all. Went in, had a look round, came out. Grand day." The tea is on the lectern, poured by Brother Quillon at ten past, still warm. "Oh, how kind. Every time! The fellow's a mind-reader." Brother Quillon says nothing, with enormous tact.

He has not looked at a clock once in anybody's sight. He does not need to. It is a quarter past four.

## The chalk

All the way down the Stair, on the wall at intervals, is a chalk mark: D., and a number, each a little deeper than the last. Between them, in the same hand, are notes, and I give the ones I have been shown, because nobody has given them before, and they deserve it:

<div class="stamp">Good boulder. Sat on it.<br>
Second-best boulder.<br>
That way, I expect.<br>
TEA. If there is any.</div>

The marks stop at D. 212. What is past D. 212 is in the article on <a href="../Places/The_Stair">the Stair</a>, and what it means for Sir Dunstable is the dispute below, and I am not going to put the two together, because both sides already have, in opposite directions, and I have nothing to add but the boulders.

## The lamp

The Door into the Mountain says NO ADMITTANCE WITHOUT A LAMP, and the village lamp has hung on his belt for as long as anybody can remember, with a note in chalk on the empty hook: *Borrowed lamp. Back directly. - D.* He would not have dreamt of lending it while the Quest was on. "Man with an appointment. Not done." After the ending, he met the Forty-Eighth in the chapel. "Free afternoon now, I expect? Then you'll want the lamp." He unhooked it from his belt and handed it over, handle first, and asked for it back directly, there's a good fellow, because he goes in at a quarter past four. He did not say which quarter past four.

When the Forty-Eighth came back in through the porch, as people do, Sir Dunstable said, "Came in by the porch, did you? Same as me. Nothing happened, I expect." He was told to keep the lamp for now. He would want it at a quarter past four.

## The question

<div class="disputed">

Whether Sir Dunstable is alive is the one question in this encyclopedia on which I have declined, on principle and in advance, to reach a view. **There are competing theories in the world**, and the evidence for each, all of it fair and none of it conclusive, is set out at length under <a href="../Places/Hithermost#sir-dunstable">Hithermost</a>, where the village can answer for itself. In brief:

*That he is alive*: he takes the tea, and is delighted by it; he lent a perfectly solid lamp, in person, with lovely manners; the Fairholme council has him under *apologies*, not under deceased, and the council minutes everything; he is very slightly more singed each time, and the singeing is cumulative; and the Forty-Eighth's party came in by the porch as well, and nobody says they are dead.

*That he is not*: his name is all the way down the page of the Book, two hundred and twelve times, *Just in case*; he comes in by the porch, the way people come back who went through the Door and did not come out of it; the chalk stops at D. 212; Brother Quillon's number, under his breath, is in the low two hundreds; something on the Stair is always on its way up; and it is a quarter past four, every day, to the minute.

To which I add one thing for each side, which I found myself, and which I have tested on both, and neither has been able to answer. For: the boulders. Nobody sits on a boulder who does not need to. Against: the boulders. Nobody marks which boulder was the second-best unless he has sat on every one of them, many times, in the dark.

My own view is that he is a man of excellent manners, and that the question is none of my business. The betting at the Slain Wyvern is held by Sops, and the odds have not moved in two years.

</div>

## Afterwards

He comes in at a quarter past four. He signs the Book. The tea is still warm. He has not asked for the lamp back, and the Forty-Eighth has it still, and has been down the Stair three times since, on free afternoons, and says the chalk is the same cheerful hand at the bottom as at the top. The Fairholme minutes arrive at Hithermost every quarter, and are gone by the morning, and his apologies are recorded at every meeting. The council has twice moved to consider whether a shareholder in a mountain is a shareholder, and has twice deferred it, on the grounds that he might be back directly.

I asked him, on my visit, whether there was anything he would like put in his article. He thought about it. He said that he would like it to say that it was a grand day. It was. I have said so.
