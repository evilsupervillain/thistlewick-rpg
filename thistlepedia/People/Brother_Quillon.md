<p class="lede"><b>Brother Quillon</b> keeps the Record in the Chapel of Continuance at <a href="../Places/Hithermost">Hithermost</a>, beside the only door into the mountain. Travellers going in write their names in the Book, in case of misfortune; in case of misfortune they come back in through the chapel porch and write them again, and he welcomes them, every time, as if for the first time. His Order forbids exactly one thing, which is saying that he remembers. He has therefore developed the most expressive silence in the Church.</p>

## The Record

> "Welcome, traveller. Brother Quillon. I keep the Record."

The Record is the Book, which lies open on the lectern and glows, softly and evenly, with nothing to account for it. Those going into the mountain write their names in it. Asked what happens in case of misfortune: "They come back in through the porch, and write their names again." Asked whether he remembers them: "The Order forbids it, traveller. It is the only thing the Order forbids."

Then he smiles, serenely, with his eyes shut. It is a very expressive smile. It has had forty years.

A traveller who signs a second time is welcomed, and asked whether he will write his name in the Book, and Brother Quillon looks down at the page, where the name already is, and says: "...Again? Of course. Of course you will."

I have been a historian for most of my life and I think that is the best thing anybody in this encyclopedia says about the keeping of records. It is the whole of my trade, said by somebody whose trade forbids him to say it.

## The silence

He does not remember. His vows say so, and he keeps them. When a party comes back in through the porch and its name is in the Book already, he looks up, and down again, and says nothing, with enormous tact.

His theology is simple: every soul deserves a second chance. He does not add anything to that, aloud. He does not look at Sir Dunstable while he says it. He does not look at him very clearly indeed. And under his breath he says something that sounds like a number in the low two hundreds, which I have tried, on three separate visits, to hear exactly, and have not, and I have come to think that he has had forty years' practice at being not quite heard, as well.

## The tea

Every afternoon at ten past four he pours a cup of tea and sets it on the lectern. At a quarter past four Sir Dunstable comes in by the porch and finds it, and is delighted. "Oh, how kind. Every time! The fellow's a mind-reader." Brother Quillon says nothing.

The kettle is the village's, and is at the chapel so often that the tea room's card says TEAS. HOT FOOD. KETTLE PERMITTING. He has never been heard to say whom the tea is for. He has never been seen to drink it himself. It is still warm at a quarter past four. It is always still warm. Whether that is the tea, he will not say; and whether he has any view at all on the question that the village argues about, I asked him, and he smiled, with his eyes shut, for some time, and that was the answer, and I could not tell you which way.

## Afterwards

Nothing has changed at the Chapel of Continuance, which is what it is for. The Forty-Eighth's party went down the Stair after the Quest, with the lamp, and came back in through the porch at least once. Brother Quillon does not remember it. The Order forbids it. The Book has their names in it, more than once, and he welcomed them each time.

He has asked me to say that the chapel is open to everybody, whether or not they are going into the mountain, and that the services are continuous, and that there is tea. He asked me to say nothing else about him, and I have said a great deal, and he has read it, and returned it, and said, "Of course."
