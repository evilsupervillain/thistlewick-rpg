# From the Notebooks of P. Quill

<p class="contributor">Contributed by P. Quill, chronicler, of Thistlewick and the Slain Wyvern</p>

<p class="hatnote">Miss Quill's notebooks are in double figures, and I asked whether I might print some of them. She chose what follows, and the order, and the headings, and wrote the notes in between, in the third person, which is her house style and not a mistake of mine. I have not corrected anything, and I have been told that if I do she will put me in a verse. For her life, see <a href="Piper_Quill">Piper Quill</a>. - H.B.</p>

Piper Quill is a chronicler, of the family that has written the Committee's accounts for four generations, and the first of it to write down what actually happened. She would like that understood before anybody reads any further. What follows is true. Some of it scans.

## The first notebook

Piper Quill joined the Forty-Eighth in the Gilded Turnip, mid-verse. She had been writing the verse for a month, walking past the inn every day, which is named after the problem. She stood on nothing. She said it at volume:

<div class="verse">
<p>'And lo, the Chosen One drew near,<br>
smelling faintly of turnip, and the tavern<br>
fell silent -'</p>
<span class="attribution">P. Quill, the Gilded Turnip, the first line of the first notebook</span>
</div>

The tavern had not fallen silent. Piper Quill wrote that down as well.

The same notebook, the same evening, has two more beginnings, and she prints them because a chronicler who prints only her endings is a Quill of the old kind:

<div class="verse">
<p>'And she went WITH him, and it was...'</p>
<p>'And the Chosen One turned to his<br>
chronicler, and said unto her -'</p>
<span class="attribution">P. Quill, the Gilded Turnip; both unfinished, and both true</span>
</div>

He did not say anything unto her. He was looking for his hat.

## A rhyme for turnip

There is no rhyme for *turnip*. The whole company sat down in the Gilded Turnip one afternoon and offered her one each, and every one is in the notebook, with who offered it, and every one is wrong, and Piper Quill would like that recorded, at volume. The draft, as it stood:

<div class="verse">
<p>'And so he came home to the fields,<br>
and sat down with a single turnip,<br>
and hung his sword above the grate,<br>
and -'</p>
<span class="attribution">P. Quill, the ballad, last verse, as drafted</span>
</div>

And as it stands, nine seconds later:

<div class="verse">
<p>'And so he came home to the fields,<br>
and hung his sword above the grate,<br>
and sat down with a turnip, which<br>
was worth the journey, and the wait.'</p>
<span class="attribution">P. Quill, the ballad, last verse</span>
</div>

It was always going to be the last verse. She just had the lines the wrong way round. Everybody who spent the afternoon on it was furious. Piper Quill has asked for the nine seconds on her headstone. Not the verse. The nine seconds.

## From the road

Most of a notebook is not verse. Most of a notebook is weather, and who was cross, and how far. But some of the road went into metre whether she wanted it to or not, and here are the parts that did.

During an examination for fitness to travel, conducted in front of a witness, with a halibut:

<div class="verse">
<p>'And lo, the medic smote his<br>
knee, and the Chosen One kicked<br>
a halibut, and -'</p>
<span class="attribution">P. Quill; the Committee will strike it, and she knew, and wrote it down anyway</span>
</div>

At the Barrow of the Forty-Fourth, on the bench, afterwards:

<div class="verse">
<p>'He did not want a mound, or fame,<br>
or masons, or a curse, or chalk;<br>
he wanted, by the road they came,<br>
somewhere to sit down from the walk.'</p>
<span class="attribution">P. Quill, on the bench, facing the sea</span>
</div>

At Upper Clanging, from the ninth Cotterill, who asked the company every morning on waking whether it was spring yet:

<div class="verse">
<p>'He asked each morning, "Is it spring?"<br>
and every morning it was not,<br>
until one morning everything<br>
was spring, and Washer, on the dot.'</p>
<span class="attribution">P. Quill; Spare Cotterill has asked for a copy, and says it is untrue, which from him is a compliment</span>
</div>

## The Contest at the Sign of the Bell

Piper Quill entered Ballad (Not Sad), at Great Dunnage, before three judges, one of whom cannot hear and one of whom gives everything a two. The damage was heckling. She finished. She scored eighteen. Mr Tweddle, who does birdcalls, scored nineteen, and the thrush got a three from Miss Quayle, and the birdcalls were, in fairness, extraordinary. Piper Quill has framed the certificate. It is the first prize a Quill has ever won for writing down what actually happened, and it is for coming second, and she says that is about right.

What she sang is not printed here. You had to be in the room.

## The struck verses

The Committee strikes any verse it cannot have read aloud at the fete, and keeps them in a box behind a red curtain, and Piper Quill has read every one of them, and prints none, because they are the Committee's, and some of them are very good, and the Committee does not know which.

Verse forty-one is about a cat. It scans.

Piper Quill has been asked about verse seven by everyone she has ever met, including the compiler of this volume, at every meeting. Piper Quill's answer, which she has given every time, and which is complete, is that it is very good.

## The elegy

*Abel Thrush, Carrier*, nine lines, is on the fingerpost at Little Gore, under a little roof somebody made for it, and is printed whole in the <a href="Piper_Quill#the-elegy">compiler's article on her</a>, by her leave. She would only add that Mrs Vane asked whether it might have a bit more of a tune, and that Piper Quill laughed, out loud, at her own work, for the first time, and said: next time. There has not been a next time yet. There will be. She is working on the tune.

## The tea

Written at the Slain Wyvern, the evening the Forty-Eighth came in from the cottage of the Forty-Fifth and told the compiler the end, and the compiler filled his last page, and Piper Quill was at the next table, and wrote this, and did not show either of them:

<div class="verse">
<p>'Forty-seven did the sword. The one<br>
who asked him why, and stayed to hear<br>
the whole of it, when it was done,<br>
went home. The kettle's on, all year.'</p>
<span class="attribution">P. Quill, the Slain Wyvern</span>
</div>

## For the historian

Piper Quill and the compiler of this volume meet at the Slain Wyvern about once a month, to disagree. He says her songs are accurate and do not scan. She says his history is complete and not accurate. They are both right, and the evenings are long, and she has written him a verse, and he has asked that it be printed at the foot of this page so that he can be seen not to mind:

<div class="verse">
<p>'Hosea Bellwether, who writes it down,<br>
has never climbed a tower, or hill,<br>
or anything above the town,<br>
and has bought boots. And sits there still.'</p>
<span class="attribution">P. Quill, for H. Bellwether, March 2002</span>
</div>

<div class="marginalia">I do not mind. It is accurate. The boots are by the door. - H.B.</div>
