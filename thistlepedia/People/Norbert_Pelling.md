<p class="lede"><b>Norbert Pelling</b> is the tutorial ambush at <a href="../Places/Firstfield_Meadow">Firstfield Meadow</a>, contracted by the Guild of Guides to stand in the gap in the fence at the top of the meadow, say STAND AND DELIVER, ask for your best attack, if you would be so kind, and lose. He has done it since 1981. By the year 2000 he had lost three thousand, one hundred and five times, and had never won, which is the job, and he is extremely good at it.</p>

## The gap in the fence

<figure class="thumb right">
<img src="../images/battlers/Norbert.png" alt="Norbert in a red bandana, with a club" width="180">
<figcaption>Bandit (Tutorial), to lose.</figcaption>
</figure>

He was born in 1961, two fields over, on his father's farm, and worked it until 1981, when the Guild advertised a contractor's post at Firstfield, *Bandit (Tutorial), to lose*, and Maud Crisp, who was not yet his wife, read it out to him over the fence and said that he would be perfect, and meant it nicely. He applied. He was the only applicant. The Guild says that is lore. Maud says it is because nobody else in the county could lose with any conviction.

The sign at the gap reads AMBUSH IN PROGRESS. PLEASE QUEUE. THE AMBUSH IS CONTRACTED TO LOSE. THE AMBUSH WOULD LIKE YOU TO KNOW HE TRIES. He tries. He puts down his sandwich. He wipes his hands on his trousers. Then:

> "STAND AND DELIVER! ...your best attack, if you'd be so kind. I'm the tutorial ambush. Norbert. Pleased to meet you."

He announces every swing a turn in advance, for the education of the young. When hit, he goes down, quite convincingly: "Oh! Ooh. You've got me. Down I go. Lovely. Lovely stuff." Then he has a lie down, for twenty minutes, which is in the contract, and which he says is the best part of the job. "Mind the sandwich."

## The other way

Twice in nineteen years, a hero has lost to him. He was mortified both times.

> "Oh. Oh dear. No, that's not how it goes. Tell you what, we'll call that one a practice. Nobody saw. I won't put it on the form."

He did not put it on the form. His record remains unblemished, at no wins, and the two heroes concerned are, he says, both doing very well now, and he would not dream of naming them, and one of them sends a card at Christmas.

He has also been run from. "Very sensible. Most heroes never think of running. Come back when you're ready. I'm on till four."

## Maud

> "The wife. She packs my lunch. There's a note in it every day. She says I'm the best loser in the county. She means it nicely. I asked."

The note says LOSE WELL, LOVE. It has said that every day for nineteen years, on a slip of paper in the sandwich tin, and he has kept every one, in a biscuit box, and there are about five thousand of them, and she says that she has never once had to think what to write, which is the secret of a good marriage. She has the first loss framed, which is to say that she had a man come from the town in 1981 and paint Norbert lying down in the meadow in the gap in the fence, very peacefully, and it is over their fireplace, and visitors take it for a memorial and offer condolences, and Maud says thank you, he's at work.

<div class="marginalia">This is Maud Pelling, of Firstfield, Norbert's wife since 1983, and not Maud Penhallow of <a href="../Places/Great_Dunnage">Great Dunnage</a>, who is somebody else's understudy, and who has an article of her own. Both of them have been in the year 2000 at the edge of somebody else's fight, waiting for their moment, and both of them, I think, would like each other very much. They have not met. - H.B.</div>

## Corvin

Corvin Ash, who was in the habit of asking anybody he met in a gap whether they were his rival, asked Norbert. Norbert said, apologetically, "Oh, sorry, no. I'm booked up through the spring." Corvin Ash said that he quite understood. They shook hands. Norbert says that it was the most courteous exchange he has had at the gap in nineteen years, and that he would have been very happy to be Mr Ash's rival in the autumn, and that Mr Ash has not been back to ask, which he quite understands.

## Afterwards

He is forty, and still at the gap, and has lost about three thousand, three hundred and forty times, and still announces his swings. The Forty-Eighth's loss he has described to me as textbook. "Lovely fight. Textbook. I have a lie down after." He says that nobody will ever beat the Forty-Eighth's record at Firstfield, because the Forty-Eighth is the only one who graduated, and that it was a privilege to lose to him, and that he would do it again tomorrow, at any time before four.
