<p class="lede"><b>Hubert Weir</b> is the Curator of the Ashcombe Drainage Museum, which is free, and so is the enthusiasm. He stands in the middle of it with his hands behind his back, in a coat with gold frogging, the way a man stands in a room he is proud of, and he has every right to: he drained the new Ashcombe, fourteen hundred yards of culvert at a fall of one in two hundred, and there has not been one puddle in it since the day they went in.</p>

## The only drain

He was born in 1936 in old Ashcombe, in the valley, three doors from the only drain the village had, which had a stone over it carved DRAIN, and which was, he says, not up to it, and he knew that it was not up to it from the age of six, when he stood on the stone in a rainstorm and watched the water go round it. He went away to learn drainage, and came back, and spent thirty years telling the parish that the valley would flood, and the parish said that it had always been wet, and the blackboard in the school still has the history of the village on it: 1. IT WAS THERE. 2. IT WAS WET. 3. WE MOVED IT.

## The flood

It flooded in 1989. Mr Weir had the village out by dinner and dry by tea, by a plan he had drawn in 1961 and kept in his coat for twenty-eight years, and not so much as a hen was lost. They have the Roll. He says it was a tremendous success, and it was, and he says it the way other men talk about a battle they won.

The village moved up the hill, and he drained it. Fourteen hundred yards of culvert, every one named in copperplate on the great plan in the museum. The longest is called Hubert. Grown men have stood in front of that plan and gone quite quiet.

<div class="marginalia">The first exhibit is the stone from over the old drain, carved DRAIN, and the card beside it, in his hand, says kindly: <i>It was not up to it.</i> I asked him whether he bore it any ill will. He said, none whatever; that it had done its best, in impossible circumstances, for two hundred years, and that he had put it first in the museum because everything after it is only what it would have done if it could. - H.B.</div>

## The Flood Dinner

Every year there is a Flood Dinner at the church hall, with soup, a fish, a pudding and speeches, and the toast is TO THE WATER, WHICH WAS VERY GOOD ABOUT IT. The menus are pinned up in the museum, and at the foot of each is the speaker: the Curator; the Curator; <a href="Mrs_Ash">Mrs Ash</a>, on knitting; the Curator; the Curator. The notice for the year 2000 read SPEAKER: TO BE CONFIRMED, and under it, in pencil, *Mr Weir has kindly offered. Again.*

It was confirmed: C. ASH. And under it, in pencil, *Mr Weir has kindly offered. Next year.* He did not mind in the least, he says; it was a very good speech, and the boy brought the name back, and that is worth more than culverts, which he does not say lightly.

## Afterwards

He is sixty-five. He spoke at the Flood Dinner of 2001, on culverts, for some time. The empty plinth in the museum still reads THE FLOOD (ON LOAN TO THE LAKE), and he says that the loan is indefinite and that the lake is a very good custodian. He has drawn a new plan, which is in his coat, for what to do if the lake should ever want to come back up the hill, and he says that it will not, but that he has been wrong about water exactly once in his life, in 1936, when he thought the stone was keeping up.
