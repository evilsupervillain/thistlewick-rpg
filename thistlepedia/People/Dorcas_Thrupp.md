<p class="lede"><b>Dorcas Thrupp</b> keeps the <a href="../Places/The_Slain_Wyvern">Slain Wyvern</a> on the front at Nether Sopping, where every hero who was ever sent to the coast has come in out of the weather, and she has kept it for longer than she will say. She is the elder sister of Prudence Thrupp of the Gilded Turnip, whom she did not speak to for thirty years. There is a wyvern over her mantel. It is a goose. She knows. It is a wyvern.</p>

## The Thrupps

She was born in 1948 at the Gilded Turnip in Thistlewick, where her father kept the house, the elder of two girls, and was brought up behind a bar, as was Prudence, three years later. She says that she learned to count on the slate and to read on the labels, and that by the age of ten she could tell you, from the way a man came through the door, what he would drink, how much, and whether he would be sorry; and that Prudence could do it by eight, and has never let her forget it.

In 1971 the Slain Wyvern at Nether Sopping wanted a landlady, the old one having gone to live with her son, and Dorcas went south to take it, forty miles, with a box of labels and her father's second-best slate. Thistlewick did not mention it. Thistlewick has never mentioned it. It was, she says, the only time in her life she has been glad of the place.

"Mrs Thrupp" is a courtesy. In Nether Sopping any woman who keeps a public house past the age of forty is Mrs, whether there was ever a Mr or not, on the grounds that she has earned it, and that the regulars need something to call her when they want another one. There was not ever a Mr. She would like that to be the end of the matter, and it is.

## The thirty years

She and her sister stopped speaking in 1970. Neither of them will say why, and neither of them, I am now fairly sure, can remember. "That's not a falling-out," says Prudence. "We just stopped one day and neither of us started again." So they sent each other things instead: by carrier, by the occasional hero going north or south, by anybody who would take a parcel and not ask. The locket that they had had made at the Michaelmas fair in 1962, two portraits in it facing resolutely away from each other, went into Prudence's drawer, and stayed there.

In the year 2000 the Forty-Eighth brought her a jar from Thistlewick, warm on one side, which he had been told not to shake or ask about. She knew the hand on the label at once. "That's my sister's hand on that label. Give it here." She looked at what was in it. "Thirty years. THIRTY. And she sends me THIS." And then: "Right. You'll be going back north. Wait there." What she sent north was heavier, and deliberately worse, and had been under construction behind the bar for some time, against exactly this day. "Don't open it. Don't shake it. Don't let it near a fire. And tell her I send my love, because I do, and she knows I do, and that's her lot."

<div class="marginalia">What was in either jar is not known, and both sisters have asked me not to guess, and I have not, except privately, at some length. The jar from Thistlewick is on the highest shelf behind the bar, where it can be seen from every table in the room, and it is still sealed, and nobody in the Wyvern has ever asked what it is, because they have all been told not to, and they all know who by. - H.B.</div>

The locket came south a little after, by the same hand: "That was HER idea, that was." Prudence wrote, at the weekend, as she said she would, four pages, two of them about the Forty-Eighth, and one of those complimentary. Dorcas wrote back. They have written every month since. They have not, so far as I know, met. Dorcas says there is no hurry, and that you do not want to rush a thing like this after thirty years, and that she is thinking about Christmas, in a year or two.

## The goose

Halloran Vance, the Forty-First, came home from the marshes in 1940 with nothing, and a whole town waiting on the quay for a monster, so he made them one, in two days, with wire, and a goose, and never once smiled about it. It has been over the mantel of the Slain Wyvern since. It was there when Dorcas came in 1971, and she has kept it as she found it, as a wyvern, and has told the story of it the same way to every hero who has asked, and every hero has asked.

> "He was a good man, and he needed it to be a wyvern, and it's been a wyvern in my house for sixty years. So."

When Wren Halloway said in front of the whole room what it was, Dorcas asked her to leave. "Out. Not for good. Just out, for a bit, while I decide." She decided. When Miss Halloway came back with a plaque of her own, in brass, not paper, because this is a house, Dorcas read it first, twice, and said nothing, and put it up herself. Both plaques are on the wall. She dusts them both on the same morning. Miss Halloway sits under the goose now, by choice, and is served without remark, and Dorcas says that is all that is being said about it.

## The house

Her rooms are the best value on the coast and she will tell you so. "Twelve hours you slept. Twelve! And up like new. Adventurers. It's the bedding I mind. You lot heal in it and it never recovers." One bed upstairs has a dip in the middle exactly the shape of a man in full plate, and she has tried everything on it, and the bed remembers him. Her bottles behind the bar are labelled in her own hand with what is in them, what it costs, and who in the room it is not to be served to; there is a name on every one.

She has views on most of her guests and shares very few of them. Of the newlyweds in room four, who had not been down since Tuesday: "Best guests I have ever had. No trouble. No noise to speak of. I leave the tray on the mat. Lovely to see newlyweds happy." Of Gerald, who proposed to a young lady in the bar every Sunday for a year, on one knee, at lunch: "We handled it. That is all that is being said about Gerald."

## Afterwards

She still keeps the Wyvern, and still says it is a wyvern. Room four has a line through it on the board, still. Sops leaves the tray on the mat and goes. The jar is still on the top shelf. The letters from Thistlewick come on the first Thursday of the month, and she reads them behind the bar, where the regulars can see her not smiling. Asked at Christmas 2001 whether she had thought any further about a visit, she said that she had thought about it a great deal, and that Prudence would have to come to her, being the younger, and that she had written to say so, and that Prudence had written back to say exactly the same thing, the other way round, and that they were, she believed, getting somewhere.
