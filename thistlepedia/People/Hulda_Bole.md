<p class="lede"><b>Hulda Bole</b> was, for eleven years, the person you sent for things. She fetched four hundred and six things, for whoever asked, from wherever they were, and wrote each one down. She told her story to the Forty-Eighth in the <a href="../Places/The_Slain_Wyvern">Slain Wyvern</a> at <a href="../Places/Nether_Sopping">Nether Sopping</a>, where she sits with the ledger and a stub of pencil, totting something up and not liking it. It was one of the six tales I paid for, and it is the one I think about most.</p>

## The list

> "Twelve bear pelts. That was the job. Fine. Went and got twelve bear pelts. Comes back. He says: ah, no. A DIFFERENT sort of bear."

The ledger is nine pages long, and every line is a thing somebody wanted fetched. It has three columns in the section about the pelts: BEAR, BEAR (DIFFERENT SORT) and BEAR (THE FIRST SORT AFTER ALL). The third column has a great many crossings-out in it, and one very firm full stop. She did not tell me who the client was. I do not think she knows his name. She says that most of them did not leave one, and that it did not matter, because it was never about who.

Asked whether she ever found out what any of it was for:

> "For? It wasn't FOR. That's the bit nobody tells you. It was a list. I was the legs."

She has nothing against the people who asked. She says that most of them were perfectly pleasant and paid on time. It is only that nobody, in eleven years, ever told her what the thing was for, and after the first few hundred she stopped asking. Her advice to anybody starting out: "If anyone asks you to fetch twelve of anything, ask what the thirteenth is for."

## The last job

Her last job was also her first. In 1989 a client of fifteen gave her a parcel in brown paper and string, to be delivered when the client was twenty-six, to the address given. She wrote it into the ledger as JOB 1. She carried it, between the other four hundred and five, for eleven years, and in the year 2000 she took it to the address given, and the client had moved, with no note. She wrote MOVED on the docket in capitals and put the parcel under the bench, and that was the end of fetching.

Later that year the client came into the Wyvern, which was <a href="Nix">Nix</a>, with Hulda Bole's good boots, which had been left outside to air in 1989 and taken, by Nix, to be aired. They were the fourth line on the first page of Nix's ledger. "My good boots?" said Mrs Bole. "I've done four hundred and six jobs in the other pair." Nix said that she knew; she had written them down. They looked at each other's ledgers. Give or take the handwriting, it was the same book. Then Mrs Bole got the parcel out from under the bench and handed it over, and said: "Here, then. You're the legs now."

Neither of them knew, at the time, that it was addressed to Nix. Mrs Bole had always assumed the client was a he. She still says he. Nix has not corrected her, and nor have I.

<div class="marginalia">When I was getting this article up I asked Mrs Bole whether she would fetch me the Wyvern's copy of the tide tables from the bar, which was eleven feet away. She looked at me for some time and asked what it was for. I said it was for the article. She said, "That's better," and went and got it, and wrote it in the ledger, on a new page, as job four hundred and seven, PURPOSE: GIVEN. - H.B.</div>

## Afterwards

She is thirty-four. She does not fetch any more, except for people who say what it is for, which in two years has been four people. Mr Bole, who fishes, says that he was never once asked to fetch anything in eleven years, being on a boat, and that he has started to feel left out. She wears the good boots every day. She keeps looking at them. On Sundays she walks, in them, along the front and back, with nothing in her hands, and nothing on a list.
