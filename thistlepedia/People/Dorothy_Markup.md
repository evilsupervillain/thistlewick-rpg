<p class="lede"><b>Dorothy Markup</b> keeps the ledger at Ye Final Shoppe in <a href="../Places/Penultima">Penultima</a>, and every page of it balances, and in the margin of every page, very small, she has written G. WAS HONEST TODAY. A few of them have a question mark. Her husband brings her tea every morning, and then he does the window, and in thirty-three years he has never once had to be asked. He has never sold the sword.</p>

## The ledger

She was born in 1946 at Haverleigh, the daughter of the clerk of the corn exchange, and was keeping her father's books by fourteen, and correcting them by fifteen, and he says she was right every time. She came to Penultima in the spring of 1969, at twenty-three, to see the Tower, which everybody in the south went to look at once, from a safe distance, and she stopped at the last shop on the way, which had been open a week.

She did not, she says, go any further up the road. She was married to the shopkeeper the spring after, and has kept the shop's books since, in a hand very like her father's, in a plain dark dress, in gold wire spectacles, the same as her husband's, which was the one deliberate thing about the wedding.

> "A boot came in this morning. One crown. It will go out at forty. That is the trade, and I write down both of them."

She writes down both of them. The difference between them is Gideon's season, and she has never once called it anything else in the ledger, and has never once failed to write G. WAS HONEST TODAY in the margin after, very small, because, she says, he was: he told the customer it was forty, and it was. The question marks are for the days when he told a customer that something was the last one.

## The window

> "He is in the window again. He is always in the window."

> "He brings me my tea every morning, and then he does the window. Thirty-one years, and never once has he had to be asked."

> "Mind you, he has never sold the sword."

I asked her, as the historian of the thing, whether he had ever lowered a price. She said, "Once." I asked what. "Eleven coppers, off a lamp, to a girl who would not stop haggling." And then, after a moment: "I have still got them."

<div class="marginalia">The entry is the first in the first volume of the accounts, in his hand, dated the first week of the shop: DISCOUNT - ELEVEN COPPERS - WORTH IT. She has ruled round it, twice, in her own ink, at some later date, which is the only time in thirty-three volumes that she has ruled round anything. I have not asked her who the girl was. I would not dream of it. She was twenty-three that spring, and she came up the road to look at the Tower, and stopped at the last shop, and did not go any further. - H.B.</div>

## Afterwards

She is fifty-six. Trade is up since 2000, and the ledger has had to go to a second volume a year, and the margins are as full as ever, and there are fewer question marks, she says, now that he has a souvenir shelf, because a souvenir is honestly priced at whatever anybody will pay for it, and that is, she says, the first honest thing about pricing he has ever discovered, and she is very proud of him. The tea still comes every morning, before the window. The lamp is still on her side of the bed. It has never been for sale.
