<p class="lede"><b>Sister Constance Bewick</b> of the Order of Whatever Works found, in the early 1990s, that the Order had no doctrine, and wrote one, and was examined on it, and passed, and dyed the trim of her habit black, and led a party of the Sisters across the ward under the name of What Ought To Work. In the year 2000 she lost to the Order, nineteen to one, on points, and then asked to be examined on its doctrine, which was four lines long, and passed, and said that she could find nothing wrong with it, and that that was very annoying.</p>

## The chemist's daughter

She was born in 1962 at Haverleigh, the chemist's daughter, and grew up behind a counter of labelled bottles, every one with its authority, and says that by ten she could not see a cure without wanting to know where it came from. She came into the Order at twenty-one, because it healed people, and found, to her very great distress, that it could not say why. Its whole Rule was four lines long, and one of them was about socks.

## The Correct Treatment

She set out to supply the deficiency. Her *Correct Treatment of the Weeping Bottom Ague* is in eleven stages, each with its authority, cited, and stage eleven reads *The patient will now feel better*, underlined twice. Toast is permitted at stage nine. Her notes are in eleven volumes, one for each stage, and volume eleven is very thin, because, she says, by stage eleven there is not very much to say, and it is the volume she is proudest of.

She has a splinter in writing as the way the ague starts. She has, she says, never been sure that it is, but that it is the earliest stage of anything she has been able to find, and that a treatment has to start somewhere, and that the patient is generally glad to have the splinter out in any case.

## The east door

In 1994 she and nine Sisters asked to be examined on the doctrine, and were, and passed, and dyed their trim black, and took the east half of the ward, and called themselves What Ought To Work. There was one door. The Prioress held symposia on the matter. The patients crossed the line at night, because there was soup on the west side, and Sister Bewick lost eleven in one month to the other half of the ward, and wrote each one down, under *Defected (soup).*

## The queue

In the year 2000 there was a queue at the gate, numbered, in the wet, and twenty patients to each half, and Whatever Works was through its twenty while What Ought To Work was on patient one, stage eleven. Patient one was Mr Grebe, a fisherman, who had the Weeping Bottom ague, properly, the first case anybody had seen in nine years, and at stage eleven he sat up and felt better.

Sister Bewick said, *It worked.* <a href="Merribell">Merribell</a> said of course it worked, he had it, and that was a point to the east, fair's fair.

Then she asked to be examined on the Order's doctrine. The Prioress took the pamphlet out of a drawer. Sister Bewick read it twice, and was examined on it, and passed, and said that it was very short, and that she could find nothing wrong with it, and that that was very annoying. There was soup on both sides of the line at three. She had some. She wrote in her notebook: *Intervention. Supported.*

<div class="marginalia">I asked her whether she had un-dyed the trim. She said she had not; that black trim is very practical in a ward; and that the Order of Whatever Works has no rule against it, or indeed against anything, which she had found, in thirty years, to be its most irritating and most admirable quality, in that order. - H.B.</div>

## Afterwards

She is forty. Whether there is one Order again, or two that agree, nobody at Weeping Bottom will say. She has added a twelfth volume to her notes, which is blank except for the four lines of the Rule of Life, copied out in her hand, with a citation under each: *Pym, 1702. Supported.* She is, Merribell tells me, the best nurse at Weeping Bottom, and the most careful, and the only Sister in the Order who can tell you exactly why the soup works, and she has asked me to say that she still does not like it.
