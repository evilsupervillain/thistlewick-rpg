<p class="lede"><b>Hester Vane</b> of <a href="../Places/Little_Gore">Little Gore</a> is plainly delighted to see anybody, because nobody comes. She has been to Fenmarch, and that is the furthest; she was married there, in the summer of 1991, and the whole of Little Gore walked over for it, all eleven of them, in their best, in the rain, and nobody minded at all, and nobody had been invited. The invitation came in the year 2000, by hand, by a knight, on his first errand. It is on the mantelpiece.</p>

## Little Gore

She was born Hester Plumb in 1967, in the end house of the hamlet's single street, and grew up in it, and has lived in it since, and has been, in thirty-four years, to Fenmarch Hall and back a great many times and to nowhere else at all, and does not see any reason to. Little Gore is eleven people, a green, a fingerpost and a log with daisies on it, and she says that it has everything you could want, and that if it wants anything else, it is four miles to Fenmarch.

## The wedding

She married Tom Vane, the under-gardener at Fenmarch, in the summer of 1991, at Fenmarch, because Little Gore has no church. The banns were read three times, and are still pinned up on the board on the green, HESTER PLUMB AND THOMAS VANE, THIRD TIME OF ASKING, and under them, INVITATIONS TO FOLLOW.

They did not follow. The invitations were with Sir Aldric Pemberton-Gore the Second, at Fenmarch, who gave them, the morning before, to his son, the Third, to carry to Little Gore as his first errand, and the Third went out errant that morning and did not find the way, and went on not finding it for nine years. Little Gore did not know any of this. It walked over anyway, in the rain, because nobody had told it not to.

> "The whole of Little Gore walked over for it. All eleven of us, in our best, and it rained the whole way, and nobody minded at all."

## The letter

In the year 2000 a man in armour came up the street to her door and took off his helmet, which nobody had ever seen him do, and held out a letter in both hands. SIR ALDRIC PEMBERTON-GORE THE SECOND REQUESTS THE PLEASURE OF THE PARISH OF LITTLE GORE AT THE WEDDING OF HESTER PLUMB AND THOMAS VANE, AT FENMARCH. She opened it on the doorstep with the children round her.

> "...It's my wedding."

He said that it was, and that he did apologise, it was his first errand.

> "Oh, but we all came anyway! All eleven of us. In the rain. Nobody had told us not to. And now we're invited. Properly. It's going on the mantelpiece. Bess, fetch the good pin."

He stopped for tea. He said that he might not find his way out afterwards. She said that then he would stop for supper. It is on the mantelpiece now, propped against the clock, where you can see it from the road, and under INVITATIONS TO FOLLOW on the banns, in a fresh hand, very neat, is FOLLOWED. (P-G.)

<div class="marginalia">Mrs Vane has written to me, to say that I should be very welcome at Little Gore, and that it was only four miles from Fenmarch, and to mind the children, who would marry me if I stood still. She enclosed a drawing by Nell, of the wedding, with all the hats much bigger, and a man in armour at the back, holding a letter, very late. I have it framed. - H.B.</div>

## Tom

> "Tom's in the gardens at Fenmarch. He walks it every day - four miles there and four back - and he says it's the best part."

Tom Vane has been at Fenmarch since he was fourteen, and head gardener since 1998, and since 1991 he has also carried the Fenmarch half of the old post round, from Little Gore to the Hall, which Abel Thrush carried for fifty years until he died that spring. Tom never stops talking, on the road, to anybody he meets, and to the hedges when there is nobody. Mrs Vane says that is why it is the best part.

## Afterwards

She is thirty-four. The children still play weddings on the green, and Bess is still the bride, or Mum, which is the same thing, and Alfie still stands there and looks pleased, and Nell is still everybody else. Since 2000 there has been a new part in the game, played by whoever is smallest: a knight, at the back, very late, holding a letter. It is the most sought-after part. They take turns.
