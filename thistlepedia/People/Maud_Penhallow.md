<p class="lede"><b>Maud Penhallow</b> has been <a href="Roland_Fairweather">Roland Fairweather</a>'s understudy since she was nine, in Fellow Blunt's yard at <a href="../Places/Great_Dunnage">Great Dunnage</a>, learning his sword, his horse, his lineage and the way he stands, in case. She is the only person who ever went to see the end of one of his engagements, and the only person who ever told him how one ends.</p>

## The yard

She went into the yard in 1993, at nine, and was told that the prophecy was nearly ready. It had been nearly ready since Roland was twelve. She learned all of it: sword, horse, lineage, the lot. In case. She learned how he stands; she says that he stands differently when he is thinking, and that you can tell.

She had never met him. When she did, in the year 2000, at the Bell, she said "Fairweather," and Roland's face did something very careful. She told him who she was. She asked him whether it was nearly ready, which had been her question since she was nine, and there was a pause of the kind that has eleven minutes in it. She asked to be told about the meetings, since she was not on the agenda. He told her. Then she asked him to look at her guard, and he did, for an hour, in the yard behind the Bell, and said almost nothing, which is how a good teacher looks at a guard.

## The question

Then she asked him something else, which nobody in twenty-two years of meetings had asked, and Roland opened his mouth, and closed it.

I have the question. I am not going to print it. It is hers, and she asked it of him, and he has not answered it, and I am not going to have it answered in an encyclopedia by somebody who was not there. I will say only that it was a good question, and short, and that the grown-ups had had twenty-two years to think of it and had not.

## The door

She said that somebody ought to see the end of one. Eleven doors, he had said; he had left at the door of every one. What she did about it is the afternoon about which this article is vague, and I give both accounts, because I have both, and the people who gave them to me were all there.

In one, she came north, and when Roland left the Forty-Eighth's company forty paces short of the Obligatory Tower, as he always does, she was there, beside the door, with a practice sword, and raised it to him, the way you are taught to at nine. "When you come out, I'll go and tell him how it ends." When the Forty-Eighth came out, she went, the whole way south, and told Roland all of it, in order.

In the other, the Forty-Eighth had already been to the door by the time she asked where this one ended, and Roland said that he had left at the door; he always does. So she asked how it ended, and the Forty-Eighth told her, at the Bell, and it took the rest of the pie, and Roland listened to all of it with his chin on his hand and did not say a word. Then Maud said that she would go and see one for herself. Not the end. The bit after.

In both, Roland laughed, properly, out loud, which nobody at the Bell had heard him do. In both, it was the first time anybody had told him the end of one.

## Afterwards

She is eighteen. She is in the yard at Great Dunnage still, between, she says, which is a word she has borrowed from Roland and is using better than he does. She still practises his guard, and has improved it, and he has looked at it, and said almost nothing, and she knows what that means.

Roland's prophecy was discharged under Carn Drey, with help, in 2000, and nobody needs an understudy for a thing that is done; she stayed in the yard anyway, and Fellow Blunt has not asked her to leave, and has, I am told, started asking her opinion. Roland has finished two further things since 2000, and keeps a list, and she has a copy of the list, and is on it, and will not tell me under what.

The question has not been answered. She says she is in no hurry. She says the bit after is very quiet, and she likes it.
