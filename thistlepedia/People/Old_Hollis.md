<p class="lede"><b>Amos Hollis</b>, known as <b>Old Hollis</b>, stands by the tent at Station One of <a href="../Places/Firstfield_Meadow">Firstfield Meadow</a> and gives every hero a stick. He has said *It's dangerous to go alone! Take this* eleven thousand, two hundred and six times in forty-one years, adjusting it for the size of the party, and not one stick had ever come back, until the Forty-Eighth brought his back, and Old Hollis shouted for his wife across the meadow at the top of his voice.</p>

## Old

He was born in 1925 in the cottage behind the tent, which was his father's, who kept sheep in the meadow before the Guild took it over, real sheep, which the Guild has never been able to get since. He was a hedger for twenty years. In 1959 the Guild of Guides advertised a post at Firstfield, *Old Man (Items)*, and he applied, at thirty-four, and was told that he was very young for it, and that he would grow into it. He has been Old Hollis since his first morning, and says that it took him about ten years to grow into it, and that he has since grown out the other side.

## The stick

Every hero who comes into the meadow is met by Old Hollis with a stick, and the words, which he says are the Guild's, not his: *It's dangerous to go alone! Take this.* For a party, he adjusts. "It's dangerous to go- ...there's a few of you, isn't there." The Forty-Eighth's party was a three, and then, when he came back up the meadow, a four, and he was given the line twice, adjusted twice, without complaint.

> "Forty-one years I've stood here. Eleven thousand, two hundred and six sticks. And not one has come back."

They go into cupboards. "Every hero's mother has a cupboard, and in it is a stick. I've seen them. Hundreds." I asked him how he had seen them. He said that heroes' mothers write to him, now and then, to thank him, and to ask what the stick is for, and that he writes back to say that it is for going alone, and that they always write back to say that their son did not go alone in the end, and that they have put the stick in the cupboard, in case.

## Nell

> "My Nell cuts them. Every evening, by the fire, forty-one years. She picks out the straight ones. She says a hero deserves a straight stick."

Nell Hollis, born Nell Coombe in 1929 at the mill at the bottom of the meadow, married him in 1951, and has cut every stick he has ever given away, about two hundred and seventy a year, from the hazel along the hedge he used to lay. She sorts them by the fire into straight and not straight, and the not-straight ones go on the fire. She is small, and round, and has never once been in the meadow during working hours, because she says it is his meadow and her fire. I asked him whether she was, as I had heard, very particular. "She is. It's why I married her. Well. That and the sticks."

## One came back

In the year 2000 the Forty-Eighth, at the end of his tutorial, walked back down the meadow and held the stick out to him. Old Hollis looked at it. He looked at it for a long time.

> "You're... you're bringing it BACK?"

> "NELL! NELL! ONE'S COME BACK!"

She came out of the tent, where she had never been during working hours, at a run, which I am told nobody at Firstfield had ever seen, and inspected it, and went back in, and came out with a straighter one. "She says you're to have this one. It's straighter." And then: "She's putting yours on the mantelpiece."

<div class="marginalia">I have been to the cottage, and seen the mantelpiece, by invitation, for tea. On it, until the year 2000, there was a photograph of Hollis on his first day, aged thirty-four, already called Old, and nothing else, because Nell does not hold with ornaments. Now there is the photograph, and the stick. She dusts the stick twice a day. "First thing on that mantelpiece in forty-one years that isn't me," said Hollis, and Nell said that was not quite true, because there had been a clock once, in 1966, but that it had not lasted, and that the stick would. - H.B.</div>

## Afterwards

He is seventy-six, and still at Station One, and still Old, and still adjusts the line for parties. He has given eleven thousand, seven hundred and forty sticks, to my last count, and two have now come back, the second from a hero who heard the story and walked back down the meadow on purpose, which Hollis says does not count, and which Nell has put on the mantelpiece anyway, at the other end, a little lower.
