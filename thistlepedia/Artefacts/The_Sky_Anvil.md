<p class="lede">The <b>Sky Anvil</b> is a round grey stone which fell out of the sky in 1749 into the top field of a farm in the snowy north, and was set up by the farmer as a gatepost, because it was there, and was a very good gatepost for two hundred and fifty years. Pilgrims built a shrine round the foot of it and come up every summer to look at it. In the year 2000 it was traded for a gate. It is now a sword.</p>

## The gatepost

The stone came down out of the sky into the Meeches' top field in 1749, on a Tuesday. Elias Meech's great-great-grandfather set it up for a gatepost, because it was there, and it was the best gatepost in the county. It outlasted eleven gates. Mr Meech never threw a gate out: all eleven were in the hedge-bottom along the top field, where each was put when it went, rusting quietly in order.

In 1750 the pilgrims came. They call it the Sky Anvil, and they built a little stone shrine round the foot of it, so that the stone sat in the top of the shrine like an egg in an egg cup, and they came up every summer to look at it. Mr Meech did not mind. It kept the frost off.

He would not sell it. It was not for sale. It was a very good gatepost. He would take a gate for it, mind; the last one had gone at Michaelmas, and he was between gates. Asked what he would hang a gate on, then, he said the shrine, which was a very good shrine.

## The trade

In the year 2000 <a href="../People/Hob_Grumnir">Hob Grumnir</a> came up the road with the Forty-Eighth's company, and stopped at the shrine, and put his hand flat on the grey stone in the top of it, the way you would put it on a horse. He had lately become quite good at gates, having mended the churchyard gate at home, and he had in his pocket the spring that had been in his pocket since the churchyard.

He had the eleven gates out of the hedge-bottom, one after another, and sorted them on the snow into what would do and what would not, and worked the afternoon through, cold, with what he carried in his pack, and made a gate out of eleven gates, true on its hangings. Then he fitted the spring to the latch. Mr Meech hung it on the shrine himself. It swung to, and the latch dropped, and stayed.

Then Hob lifted the Sky Anvil out of the top of the shrine like an egg out of an egg cup, and put it under one arm. It is very heavy. He did not mention this.

## The sword

Star-iron makes exactly one thing. Hob knew it would. He put it in the fire at his own forge in Thistlewick and said nothing for a long time, which for Hob is about a minute, and made a sword: plain, long in the blade, that takes the light like frost on a window.

Hob cannot use a sword. Everybody in the smithy knew it, and nobody said so. Asked who it was for, he said nobody; it was a sword, and swords are for whoever. Then he listened to the hammer at his belt for a while, and said that she says a sword is no use on its own, and that it was not on its own. He wrapped it in sacking and put it in the Forty-Eighth's pack, and did not look at anybody, and went to see about the fire.

He has not said who it is for. I have a theory, and I have been asked not to print it, by Hob, and by Beatrice, through Hob, and by two other people who have the same theory. I am not printing it.

## Afterwards

The shrine has a gate on it now, and nothing in the top. The pilgrims still come up every summer, and open the gate and shut it all afternoon, because nobody had ever given them anything to open before. Mr Meech says that it is the best gate in the county, and leans on it.

<div class="marginalia">Nix's ledger has the star-iron in it, because it went through the back room on its way to the forge, overnight, wrapped in a sack. The entry reads: <i>One stone, sky. Traded, not located. From E. Meech, for a gate, fair. Held overnight for H. Grumnir. Returned.</i> She showed me the word RETURNED, and pointed out that it is in the right column. - H.B.</div>
