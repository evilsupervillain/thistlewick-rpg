***Bramus agricola*** (Halloway, 2000). A small brown burrowing animal of turnip fields. New to science in the year 2000, although not new to the field, which has always had them.

**What it is.** A small brown animal, which lives in a burrow in the turnip rows, with a neat round entrance and a rim of turned earth. Nobody has ever described one. Nobody had ever looked. They are all over the field. I am not able, yet, to place it in a family; it is brown, and it is small, and it lives in turnips, and every one of those is true of several families. I have described what I saw, and named it, and the rest is for volume two.

**What it is called.** *Bramus agricola*, after the Forty-Eighth, in whose field it was found. There are no synonyms. Nobody else ever named it, because nobody else ever noticed it, which is the first thing it has in common with its namesake and not the last. In Thistlewick it is called "one of them things", which is not a name.

**Where.** Turnip fields about Thistlewick; the type locality is the Thistle field, one row in from the lane, where a labelled stick marks the burrow:

<div class="stamp">BRAMUS AGRICOLA (HALLOWAY). DO NOT PLOUGH. IT WILL NOT HELP.</div>

**Description.** Small. Brown. Distinguishing features: none. Goes pink when it is looked at.

**Diet.** Turnip.

**Predators.** Everything.

**Behaviour.** Of no consequence whatsoever, except in one attribute, which is that it survives things it has no business surviving. By luck. Repeatedly. I observed the type specimen for something under a minute. In that time it came out of its burrow, looked at nothing in particular, and walked directly into the path of a wheelbarrow. The barrow went over it. It came out of the other side of the barrow a little flatter, shook itself, and carried on. A crow dropped on it from a height, missed, hit the barrel, and left with a great deal of dignity. The animal did not notice either event. I have called this, in print, statistically indefensible, and I stand by it.

**Method.** None was needed. It was not fought, because it did not know that it was in anything, and it was not measured, because it would not keep still, and it was not collected, because I did not have the heart, and because it would have got away, by luck. It was observed, and described, and named, and left in its burrow. I am aware that this departs from my own practice for a type specimen. The holotype is the one in the burrow. It is still there.

**Etymology.** *Bramus*, for Bram Thistle of Thistlewick, Chosen One #48, a turnip farmer, who was chosen while he was asleep and therefore unable to object, and walked north, and walked into very nearly everything, and came home. *Agricola*, a farmer. I named it after him because the description fitted, and I told him so, and why, and watched him think about the <a href="../Places/The_Gloamwood">Gloamwood</a> and the <a href="../Places/The_Obligatory_Tower">Tower</a> and everything since the vote, and how much of it he had walked away from, and why. He went pink. Then he said nothing at all for some time, and had to sit down on a turnip. I wrote that down under his name. He would have liked to discuss which name, and could not find a way to begin.

I want it understood that it is not a joke. Nobody had ever described him accurately before. It is an accurate description.

**Remarks.** The Prophecy does not read journals. It has, I am told, read this one; its own account of the Quest mentions the animal by name, and spells it correctly.

<div class="marginalia">The stick is still there, and the hole, and I am told by the Forty-Eighth that the animal is too, or one very like it, and that he ploughs round it. I asked him whether he had ever seen it hurt. He said no. I asked whether he thought that was luck. He said that he was not the person to ask. - H.B.</div>
