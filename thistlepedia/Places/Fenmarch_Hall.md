<p class="lede"><b>Fenmarch Hall</b> is a grey house behind a low wall, with its roofs gone green and a moat all the way round it, on a headland in the far south-east with the sea on both sides. Everything about it is slightly wet. It is the seat of the Pemberton-Gores, a family which has always faced the wrong way. It is on the arms.</p>

## The gate

Two stone gateposts, green with damp, carry the family arms, a knight in armour rearing up and looking back over his shoulder at something behind him, and the motto, RESPICE. Look behind you. On the inside of the left-hand post, at about the height of a boy of ten, somebody has scratched FOUND IT.

That was <a href="../People/Pemberton-Gore_IV">the Fourth</a>, Sir Aldric's nephew, who found the gate first time when he was ten, and is the first of the family who ever has. His grandfather, Sir Aldric the Second, came over the wall until he was eighty, and said that it was quicker. It was not. He simply could not find the gate. The house is easy enough to find. It is the gate that the family has trouble with.

The moat goes all the way round the house, and in places a little further than that: the lawn is wet for twenty yards in every direction, and so is the house. There are mushrooms on the lawn, which is also, in places, the moat, and a ring of them which the gardener has bordered with stones, as if they were meant. The gardener is **Tom**, of <a href="Little_Gore">Little Gore</a>, who walks over every day, four miles both ways, and says it is not a long way when you have somebody at the other end of it. He was married at Fenmarch, eleven years ago this summer. It rained. It always rains at Fenmarch.

## The gallery

Most of the house is the long gallery. **Mrs Puddifoot**, the housekeeper, in a white cap with a duster in each hand, keeps house, what there is of it. Everything in the house is damp except the animals, because Mr Ottershaw won't have it. There are eighty of them. She starts dusting on a Friday and is done by the Wednesday after, and then it is Friday again. The cases in the gallery do not come to eighty; the rest are upstairs, one in every bedroom, and you get used to it.

**The portraits** run down one wall, and every one of them is of somebody's back. Not one of them has ever followed anybody round a room with its eyes. Sir Aldric the First, in armour, from behind, looking back over his shoulder at something outside the frame. Dame Honoria Pemberton-Gore, 1811, from behind, looking out of a window; the painter was paid for a front, and waited three weeks for one. Rollo, a dog, 1840, from behind; she was the only one of the family who could find her way home, and she did it facing the right way. Sir Aldric the Third, from behind, in armour, walking out of the picture at a good pace, with a box of fresh flowers under it that Mrs Puddifoot has changed every morning for eleven years. And at the end, the Fourth, painted in the spring of 1999, the only portrait in the gallery facing out, pointing confidently at the gate.

In the year 2000 Sir Aldric stood in front of his own portrait for some time, and said that he had been going to come back, and that he had, rather. Mrs Puddifoot changed the flowers anyway, because it was the morning, and nobody told her not to. She still does.

**The cases** line the other side, thirty-two in the gallery, each with a brass plate. THE MARSH BEAR (*Ursus fenmarchensis*), taken in the moat by Sir Aldric the Second; it had come for the crayfish, and had been at them some time. THE FENMARCH PHOENIX, which rose from the kitchen fire, and was put out by Cook. A ROOK (Scaled), shot on the lawn by Sir Aldric the First, who had been looking for the gate at the time. THE GREATER MOAT BEAR, larger than the Marsh Bear, and wetter. A HORSE, and the plate says nothing else; there is room left on it where somebody meant to, and did not like to ask. A UNICORN, of the Home Farm, bred at Fenmarch; not a success as a unicorn; it kept the foxes down. The Latin names are Dr Rowbotham's. He named the bear. He named everything. That, Miss Halloway says, was the problem.

## Mr Ottershaw

At the far end of the gallery, at a workbench among the cases, in green lenses, with a needle and a great deal of thread, sits **<a href="../People/Mr_Ottershaw">Mr J. Ottershaw</a>**, who was ninety in March of 2000 and did the animals. Did he preserve them? Anybody can preserve. A cook can preserve. He COMPOSED them. Seventy years, three generations, and the family will tell you every one of them is rare, and the family are right: there is one of each. He will ask you to look at a shoulder and tell him where it stops, and you will look for a long time, at feathers and then scales, and find nowhere at all where the one becomes the other. In seventy years, he said, nobody had ever found a join. Not one of them had ever looked.

In the year 2000 the family engaged Miss <a href="../People/Wren_Halloway">Wren Halloway</a>, a naturalist, a proper one, with the jars, to look at them properly. The Fourth was across the gallery before Mrs Puddifoot had finished asking. Three generations they had been proud of them. Wren said she would tell them what they were, all eighty, and that they might not like it. The Fourth said they would love it; they love everything; ask anybody.

Mr Ottershaw, when she told him she had been engaged to authenticate them, put down the needle, took off his lenses and put them on again to see her better, and said that in seventy years nobody had ever ASKED. He took her down the gallery at a pace you would not expect of a man of ninety, and showed her every join. The Marsh Bear is bear to the waist and crab below; the crab end came for the crayfish. The Phoenix is a salamander and the top of something that fell in Cook's fire. The Horse is crab and unicorn, and the plate is blank because nobody liked to ask what it was. She found each join only once he had put his finger on it, and wrote it down, and her hand was not quite steady. Then she read her write-up out to the household, standing, the way you would read a will:

<div class="stamp">THE FENMARCH COLLECTION. EIGHTY CASES. Seventy-nine composed, each of two animals, by Mr J. Ottershaw, 1930 to the present. None is what its plate says. Not one of them ever lived. The joins are the finest work I have seen. I have looked for joins all my life. I could not find these without being shown, and then I could not stop looking at them. Case forty: not examined. W. Halloway.</div>

The Fourth said, *None of them?* and she said none of them, she was sorry, and he said COMPOSED, by Mr Ottershaw, nobody else in the county had a COMPOSER, and that was better than real; anybody could have real. Mrs Puddifoot paid the fee out of a tin on the mantelpiece and had the write-up framed before Wren had her coat on. It hangs over the fire, and Mrs Puddifoot dusts it first, before the cases. Mr Ottershaw has a copy over his bench, with *finest* underlined twice. Wren said that nobody had ever been pleased with a write-up of hers, and that she was not sure what to do with her face.

Case forty is the Home Farm's unicorn. Mr Ottershaw went past it without looking at it, the way you go past a door you don't open. Miss Halloway stopped, and looked at the shoulder, and the horn, and the shoulder again, for a long time, and then at Mr Ottershaw, who was threading a needle, and closed her notebook, and wrote nothing, and walked on. She has not examined it since, and she will not discuss it, and I have not asked her to. It is in the write-up as she left it.

<div class="marginalia">I asked Mr Ottershaw, by letter, whether there was anything he wished to add to this article. He wrote back, in a very fine hand, that he would be ninety-two in March, that he was still composing, and that there were eighty-three now. He did not mention case forty. Nor do I.</div>
