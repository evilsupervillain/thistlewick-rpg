<p class="lede"><b>Ashcombe</b> is a village on the shore of a lake in the east. Thirteen years ago the lake came up over the old village, on a Tuesday, and every soul in it walked up the hill with the kettles, and was dry by teatime, and by teatime had decided to build it again, only better. It did. The old village is under the lake. The new one has the best drains in the world, and is extremely cheerful about all of it.</p>

## The village

The plaque is polished stone, facing the lake, with the gilt still bright: HERE, ON THE DAY OF THE WATER, EVERY SOUL IN OLD ASHCOMBE CAME SAFE UP THE HILL, AND WAS DRY BY TEATIME. WE BUILT IT AGAIN, ONLY BETTER. And under that, smaller: MIND THE DRAINS. THEY ARE VERY GOOD. VISIT THE MUSEUM! (FREE.)

Everything is new. The church is pale stone with a green roof and coloured glass in every window, and the bell is the old one, brought up the hill on a cart on the day of the water, because nobody saw why they shouldn't. It rings at six for dinner, and if you ring it at any other time a door flies open up the street and somebody shouts COMING! and then, rather put out, *Oh. It isn't six.* The church door's card says SERVICE AT SIX. KEY WITH THE BELL. The bell is at the top of the tower.

The school has a brass plate, ASHCOMBE SCHOOL, polished, and under it, also polished, *'Very Good.' - H.M. Inspector of Schools.* The blackboard has a history of Ashcombe on it: 1. IT WAS THERE. 2. IT WAS WET. 3. WE MOVED IT. On every desk there is a paper boat. **Wilf**, a small boy with another paper boat, is looking for somewhere to sail it, and there are no puddles, and Mr Weir says there will not ever be a puddle again, and says it like it's good. In one front room a line is painted on the wall at knee height, with a date beside it, and a smiley face. In another a family is at tea with every chair on little stilts. Old habits. The scarecrow wears waders and a life ring.

**The post office**, kept by **Mrs Dimmock**, answers every letter that comes to Ashcombe. It's policy. ALL LETTERS ANSWERED, says the card in the window, and under it, WE MEAN IT. Years ago they had one addressed to THE RUINS OF ASHCOMBE, and so the Ruins wrote back, a lovely long letter, hoping he was keeping warm. The post box by the door is iron, painted red, with a slot like a polite smile, and is Hob Grumnir's, and he asked for it back, a while, and Mrs Dimmock asked where the letters would go, and said that box had had a letter from the Ruins of Ashcombe in it, and you cannot take a box back after that. Hob said no, you can't.

## The Drainage Museum

The Ashcombe Drainage Museum is free, and so is the enthusiasm. **<a href="../People/Hubert_Weir">Mr Hubert Weir</a>**, Curator, in a coat with gold frogging, stands in the middle of the room with his hands behind his back, the way a man stands in a room he is proud of. Fourteen hundred yards of culvert, a fall of one in two hundred, and not one puddle in Ashcombe since the day they went in. The flood, he says, was a tremendous success: everybody out by dinner, everybody dry by tea, and not so much as a hen lost. They have the Roll.

The new village is eight miles from the old one, by the ROAD, sir. The road goes all the way round the lake. As the crow flies you could skim a stone on to the old church roof.

The exhibits are worth an afternoon. **The plan of the drains**, on a great sheet of parchment, every culvert named in copperplate; the longest is called Hubert. Grown men have stood in front of that plan and gone quite quiet. **Exhibit one** is the stone that was set in the old high street over the only drain old Ashcombe had, carved DRAIN, and the card beside it says, kindly, *It was not up to it.* **An empty plinth**, beautifully polished: THE FLOOD (ON LOAN TO THE LAKE). **Exhibits fourteen and fifteen** are a plant (dry) and another plant (also dry). **The painting**, *The Lake, at Evening*, painted for the Flood Dinner, has the old church spire painted in under the water, very small, and it is the best bit of the picture, and they know it.

**The Roll** was taken on the hill on the day of the water, in the schoolmistress's hand: every name in the old village, a tick by each, and after every tick one word, DRY. Two hundred and six ticks. Beside one name, in place of a tick, in the same careful hand: *Away. (Written to.)* That is Corvin's. Two hundred and six people stood on a hill in the rain and counted, and when they got to him they wrote down where he was, and then they wrote to him. He was in a corner of the Slain Wyvern, being away, on purpose. They counted him anyway.

## The Flood Dinner

Every year there is a Flood Dinner at the church hall, with soup, a fish, a pudding and speeches, and the toast is TO THE WATER, WHICH WAS VERY GOOD ABOUT IT. The menus are pinned up in the museum, one for every year, and at the foot of each is the speaker: the Curator; the Curator; Mrs Ash, on knitting; the Curator; the Curator. The notice for the year 2000 read: SPEAKER: TO BE CONFIRMED, and under it, in pencil, *Mr Weir has kindly offered. Again.*

It was confirmed: C. ASH. (CONFIRMED.) And under it, in pencil, *Mr Weir has kindly offered. Next year.* Two hundred and six people, and the old bell rung for six exactly. What Corvin Ash said is in his article, and I shall not shorten it here, except to say that he brought the name back, and that they may as well have it, because they were using it, and that the toast was to the water, which was very good about it. Mrs Ash cast off the last stitch in the front row, and stood, and held up a scarf that went most of the way down the aisle, bright yellow, the yellow of the new roofs, which is the wrong colour for everything Corvin owns. If she had done it in black, nobody would ever see him in it. He wears it everywhere.

He was asked back to the Flood Dinner of 2001, and went, and was early. Mr Weir spoke that year, on culverts, for some time.

## Mrs Ash

**<a href="../People/Mrs_Ash">Mrs Ash</a>** has a kitchen so new the paint still smells of it, with a big window over the lake, and she knits something long and does not stop. Mind the step. It's new. It's all new. Isn't it lovely? The old kitchen was DARK; you couldn't see your potatoes. She can see every potato she owns. Her boy wrote to say that he had taken the name Ash, after the village, in its memory, and she thought, well, that's nice, and took it as well, to save the postman, and now they are all Ashes and the post comes lovely. He is very deep, her boy. She wrote to him at the Slain Wyvern, Wednesdays and Saturdays, for eleven years, and he wrote back once, to the Ruins of Ashcombe, and the Ruins wrote back. She thought he would like that. He did.

His room upstairs is bigger now, and has the light, and is a study at the minute, where the papers of the Flood Dinner Committee are kept: Hon. Sec., Mrs Ash. Up the wall by the window are pencil marks with ages, C., 6; C., 9; C., 12; C., 16; C., 19. The old ones went under with the old wall. These were put back from memory, and every one of them is a little taller than a boy would have been, and the last is exactly the top of his head. She drew where he would be.

## Old Ashcombe

Once a year, in the week of the Flood Dinner, they let the lake down for the sluices, and the old street comes up for air, at the bottom of a slope of wet mud below the shore. On a still day, they say, you can hear the old church bell ringing under the water. You cannot. It is up the road in the new Ashcombe, calling somebody in for their dinner. They took it with them.

In sluice week the old street is mud and stones and lily pads. There is the sign of the Anchor, face up, where Corvin was not allowed in and sat outside and waited. There is a gatepost with a hinge on it that still turns. A rose has got through the gap where a gate was, and flowers on the lake side. A coot has built a nest on the old maypole stump and sits on it very seriously. Round a stump somebody has pressed a ring of shells into the mud; somebody who comes down here sometimes, and sits.

At the edge of the old street there is a doorstep with nothing behind it, and cut into it are letters in the old script, the ones on the tang of Corvin's sword, and under them, in plain capitals, for visitors, MIND THE STEP. His grandfather cut that, and made the sword as well, and it stood there forty years holding the door open. That is its name. In the schoolroom next door a strongbox held a report for every child old Ashcombe ever had, dry, in oilcloth, in the schoolmistress's hand, and Corvin's was near the top, from the last year. It is a fair report. It is one fewer in the box now.

At the end of the street the road goes on into the water, and just under the surface is a green roof: the old church, up to its eaves. You could skim a stone on to it.

<div class="marginalia">Corvin Ash showed me his report, which he carries in his coat, next to the scarf. I asked whether I might print it. He said no, and then he said that I might print the last line, and I do: *Corvin could do anything he put his mind to, and has put it to waiting. He will go far, if he ever gets up.*</div>
