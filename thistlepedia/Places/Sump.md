<p class="lede"><b>Sump</b> is a hamlet on a sandy spit on the south coast, under a sign bleached almost white by the sun. For two hundred and six years it was cursed: headaches, everybody; sleep, nobody; tempers, short; and a general sense that things used to be better. In the year 2000 a field medic asked the parish when anybody in Sump had last drunk a glass of water.</p>

## The curse

The first thing anybody sees of Sump is a pool with a palm tree leaning over it, blue and clear. It is salt. It is the sea, which comes in under the sand at high tide and goes out again at low, and somebody has stuck a little sign in the middle of it that says NO.

The parish minutes are posted. 1794. Item 1: Wasps, in the well. Whose? Deferred. 1794. Item 2: The Water Committee to be wound up, there being no water. Carried. 1795. Item 1: Wasps. Deferred. 1795. Item 2: A general sense that things used to be better. Noted. Suspected curse. And so on, every year, in six different hands, WASPS - DEFERRED, until the year 2000, when it was underlined, and the pen had gone through the paper.

Nobody could say whose the wasps were. A wasp is an animal, which is the Pound. A nest is a structure, which is Buildings. A well is Water, and the Water Committee was wound up in 1794. The parish clerk, **Mr Absalom Thurle**, in a wide hat, pressing two fingers very hard to the side of his head, had a head like a drum, and so did everybody, and nobody in Sump had had a decent night's sleep in six generations. Item one was always the curse. Item two was always the wasps, deferred. There was never an item three. There was never time.

The houses said the rest. QUIET PLEASE. TRYING TO SLEEP, on an old card, put up in hope. NO CALLERS. NO HAWKERS. NO CHOSEN ONES. THE CURSE IS QUITE ENOUGH TO BE GOING ON WITH, THANK YOU. A clay pot, empty, with a card, FOR RAIN, a very optimistic pot. A cactus with a card on a spine, THINGS USED TO BE BETTER, and it was the only thing in Sump that disagreed. In the parish hut a long table, one chair at its head, and a great many empty chairs, kept for when things got better. **Mrs Pardew** would not be talked to before noon, or after; it was the curse. **Mr Gammage** sat against a wall with his eyes shut and was not asleep, and had been trying to get off since Tuesday.

## The lifting

The whole of it is in Merribell's article, and in the minutes, and I give the shape only. Merribell asked for an item three, and was told, through the chair, that there was never time, and said there was now, and was quick: headaches, sleep, tempers, a general sense, and skin like a letter left in a window. Mr Thurle went back through the ledger, a page at a time, for some while, to find when anybody in Sump had last drunk a glass of water. It was 1794. It was not a curse. It was thirst, six generations of it. He said it was a very thorough curse. She said it was a very thorough thirst.

She moved that the Water Committee be re-formed, and the Forty-Eighth seconded, which was the first motion seconded in Sump since 1794, and Mr Thurle had to look up how it was written down. Its first business was wasps. Not deferred. Wasps want water and something sweet, and they had had the water for two hundred years, so Mrs Pardew's gran's last jar of gooseberry jam, 1931, labelled (BETTER), was opened at the far end of the spit, in the sun, and the wasps went to it, all of them, in about four minutes, extremely loudly. Nobody was stung. Nobody had ever asked them to leave.

The parish had Standing Orders for the Lifting of the Curse, drafted in 1795 and never used, and it followed them, in order. A Chosen One was present. The guardian of the well was overcome, by jam. An ancient relic was given up: gooseberry, 1931. A price was paid, in sorrow: it was the last jar. The eldest spoke the riddle; Mr Gammage opened one eye and asked what was wet, and in the middle of the village, and they had not had any since 1794, and said it had taken them six generations, that one. The curse was called by its true name. Merribell let down a coil of rope and a tin cup, which she had in her bag, of course, and it came up full, clear and cold and only very slightly wasp-flavoured.

Item three was the Rule of Life of the Order of Whatever Works, four lines, by Mother Ursula Pym, 1702, adopted as a by-law: I. Drink a glass of water. II. Sit down. III. Dry socks: two pairs, and a spare. IV. Then see how you feel. So the parish of Sump did it, by law, in order, down the street and back. Socks were the one line of the four nobody in Sump had ever had any trouble with. Then it saw how it felt. Mr Thurle took his hat off. Mrs Pardew said *Oh.* Mr Gammage was asleep, sitting up against the wall, properly, for the first time since Tuesday.

The minutes read: 2000. Item 3: The Rule. Carried, and done, in order. Under it: Felt better. And: Item one. The curse. Lifted, 2000. There was time for an item four, and nobody had one, and it was wonderful.

## Since

The sign on the spit now reads, in fresh paint under the old: LIFTED 2000. PLEASE MIND THE WELL. (RULE OF LIFE APPLIES.) The well has a rope on it, and a tin cup on the rope. Mrs Pardew can be spoken to before noon now; not after, but that is just her. Mr Thurle writes WASPS on every agenda still, and after it, NOT DEFERRED, to see how it looks. The wasps carry on at the far end of the spit, unminuted.

Sump was the far end of Abel Thrush's road, and Mr Gammage told Piper Quill that for forty years, every Tuesday, a bottle of Fenmarch water was left on their well, and nobody drank it. She put that in the elegy: *He brought the water nobody would drink.* Mrs Pardew told her that he was a terrible man for his tea, and stopped anywhere for it, and took all day to do four miles.

<div class="marginalia">I wrote to the parish of Sump, as I have written to every parish in this volume, for anything it wished to add. Mr Thurle replied for the chair. The parish wished to add that things are better, and that it is in the minutes, and that the cactus planted to cheer the place up, which has been trying for two hundred years, has flowered.</div>
