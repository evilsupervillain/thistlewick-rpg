<p class="lede"><b>Frostwick</b> is a town in the far north where it snows because the town pays it to. Forty-two years ago a man walking north broke its winter, and it thawed, for eleven days. On the twelfth day the town held a meeting, and retained a practitioner to put the winter back on a sensible footing, and asked the man to cut a ribbon. It has done both every year since.</p>

## The winter

The sign at the town's edge reads FROSTWICK. HOME OF THE WINTER FESTIVAL. SNOW GUARANTEED*, and along the bottom, much smaller: \*Under contract. Schedule of snowfalls available on request from the parish.

It is available on request. I requested it. *Contract for the Maintenance of a Winter, Appendix C: Schedule of Snowfalls.* Ordinary snow: four inches, overnight, not on market days. Blizzard: at the committee's request, in writing, one week's notice, not during carols. Festival Day: a light dusting, commencing at the ribbon, to settle on Mr Stagg's hat. Tolerance: plus or minus one flake. Somebody measures the snow on the lamp-posts, and it is exactly the contracted four inches, and the grit barrel has a sign on it saying that it is not to be used during the Festival, because the snow has been paid for.

The winter is kept by Miss **Eudora Pargeter**, retained practitioner, in the Cold Room on the square, in pale blue, with her gloves on indoors as a matter of course. She would like it known before you ask that she is not an ice witch. She does refrigeration: larders, dairies, the fishmonger's, and the winter, which is one client, and a good one. She does January in an afternoon and is home by four. Her contract is for nine years, and has been renewed on time, and she would sign it again tomorrow. Her own larder is a shelf of frosted jars, labelled JANUARY, and which week. Her box files read CONTRACT; CONTRACT (AMENDMENTS); CONTRACT (AMENDMENTS TO THE AMENDMENTS); and a slim one, COMPLIMENTS.

She is the only person in the world, so far as I have been able to find, who has read the Prophecy of Thistlewick all the way through. She read it at her desk, when the Forty-Eighth showed it to her. She said there was no break clause, and no notice period, and that clause twelve renews itself, and that she would never sign a thing like that. Then she turned back to clause two, and read out that "a hundred years" is a defined term, meaning *such period as the Committee shall find convenient*, and then she mentioned that there would be a light dusting on Tuesday, for the look of the thing. She did not do the arithmetic for him. She does not do anybody's arithmetic for them. See <a href="../History/Two_Reckonings">Two Reckonings</a>.

## The Working Group

The town is governed by the **Working Group on the Winter**, which has never had apologies for absence, because nobody in Frostwick has ever missed a meeting. It is chaired by **<a href="../People/Wilhelmina_Blagg">Mrs Wilhelmina Blagg</a>**. She will tell you that the curse is an asset. It is on page one of the register, depreciating at four per cent a year, towards spring, eventually, which has been budgeted for. The auditor called it forward-looking. He came in August. It was snowing. He was very impressed.

The register of assets reads: 1. The Winter (maintained); value, considerable. 2. The Ribbon (one). 3. The Lanterns (212). 4. Mr Stagg, which is struck through, and in the margin: NOT AN ASSET. A PERSON. - W.B.

The founding minutes are on the table, and I copy them as I found them:

> MINUTES OF THE WORKING GROUP ON THE WINTER. Extraordinary meeting, the twelfth day.
>
> Item 1. That the winter has stopped. Noted.
>
> Item 2. Takings, carols and lanterns, since: nil. Noted, with concern.
>
> Item 3. That a practitioner be retained to put it back, on a sensible footing. For, 41. Against, 3, who had planted peas. Carried.
>
> Item 4. That Mr Stagg be asked to open the first Festival. Carried unanimously, with one abstention: Mr Stagg.
>
> Item 5. Any other business. The peas. Deferred.

The peas are still deferred. There is a crate of bunting in the room marked BUNTING (CONTESTED), and the carol sheets each have a verse crossed out by the Working Group, on the grounds that it promised snow on the twelfth without consulting them.

**<a href="../People/Clem_Blagg">Clem Blagg</a>**, who was seven in the year 2000 and does the lanterns, knows all the minutes off by heart: item three, the winter, CARRIED. His view, given to the Forty-Eighth unprompted, is that Nan says Mrs Stagg has never once been cold, not in forty winters, and that it must be Mr Stagg's lanterns, because he does ever such good lanterns. I was told that, when this was said, the committee found the ceiling very interesting, and that Mrs Blagg found it most interesting of all.

## Perce and Ida

**<a href="../People/Perce_Stagg">Percival Stagg</a>**, Perce, is an old man in a red coat with a white fur collar who stands beside the ribbon post as though it might need him. He is the man who broke the winter. He was not, he will tell you, a Chosen One. Nobody chose him. Everybody went north that year, the year of the Forty-Fourth: farmers, tinkers, a man with a goat. He got as far as Frostwick, and the winter was in the way, so he saw to it. He has never mentioned that he was not chosen, because nobody has ever asked, and they were all so pleased, and it is a lovely Festival.

When a lady with a basket goes by and says *Our Chosen One!* he does a face, warm and modest and very well practised. He does it again at noon on the twelfth, when he cuts the ribbon, and the whole town cheers, and he goes pink. The ribbon is in a gilt case at the Working Group's rooms, cut and tied so often it is mostly knots: THE RIBBON. 40 FESTIVALS. 1 RIBBON. There are forty-two now.

**<a href="../People/Ida_Stagg">Ida Stagg</a>** loves the Festival. She will tell you that he did one great thing forty years ago, and a great many things since, and none of those ever got a ribbon. She asks you not to tell him she said so, because he will only go pink again.

Their house has its door shut, and a card in the window in two hands: AT THE RIBBON. - P. BACK FOR TEA. - I. Through the window there are two armchairs drawn up very close to the fire, and two pairs of slippers warming on the fender, toes touching.

The ribbon post has an iron crown on top of it, worked like frost on a window. It is <a href="../People/Hob_Grumnir">Hob Grumnir</a>'s work. In the year 2000 he asked whether he might have it back, for the spring, for a piece he had in mind at <a href="Great_Dunnage">Great Dunnage</a>. Perce said that everybody ties a ribbon to that crown, and that you cannot tie a ribbon to a bare post, you know. It slides down. Hob said that it did, aye. The crown is still on the post, and ribbons do not slide off it.

## The Eleven Days

The inn is named for the thaw. **Mrs Tibby Marchpane** will tell you about it whether you ring the bell or not; the card on the bell says RING FOR SERVICE, OR FOR THE STORY OF THE THAW, and they are, in practice, the same thing. Eleven days it lasted. Asked what happened on the twelfth, she says: the meeting. A room is eleven crowns, three quilts and a hot brick, snow included. In the morning it has snowed four inches, exactly, and not on the path. She draws a little face in golden syrup on every guest's porridge regardless of age. She drew one on mine.

By the fire is a brass plate over a line painted along the skirting: THE ELEVEN DAYS. SNOW DEPTH DURING: THIS HIGH. The line is on the floor.

The visitors' book is open on the bar under a card, SIGNED BY THREE CHOSEN ONES. PLEASE ASK. It is ruled NAME, FROM, WHITHER, and a fourth column in a newer hand, CHOSEN ONE (Y/N). There are two very old entries a century apart, both bound north, both marked Y, the names faded out; one says *Lovely weather*, and the other says *Cold*. The third is P. Stagg, of nowhere in particular, bound north, and in the last column, in Mrs Marchpane's hand, a firm Y. Under the Y, rubbed out but not quite, smaller, in his own hand: N.

The Forty-Eighth signed it too. The card has not been changed. I asked Mrs Marchpane whether it ought to say four. She said that it said three, and that it always had, and would I like the story of the thaw.

<div class="marginalia">The snowman on the green wears a Festival rosette: JUDGED - SNOW, ADEQUATE. The icicle on the tavern sign has been there so long it has a name, which is Reginald. A barrel by the Cold Room is labelled SPARE SNOW (CONTRACT), and is full, and I suspect is not, strictly, allowed. <a href="../People/Spare_Cotterill">Spare Cotterill</a> made a snowman here and wrote home that he was under contract.</div>
