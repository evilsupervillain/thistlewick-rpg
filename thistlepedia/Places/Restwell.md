<p class="lede"><b>Restwell</b> is a town of nineteen inns and one street, at a crossroads, where a traveller goes upstairs and sleeps eight hours and comes down whole, warm, unpoisoned and agreeing without discussing it that it is morning. That is the arrangement in the songs. In Restwell it is also the arrangement, and it takes about seven minutes.</p>

## The town

The fingerpost at the crossroads has four boards. Three say RESTWELL. The fourth says RESTWELL (CONT.) and points back the way you came. Under them, smaller: NINETEEN INNS. ONE STREET. PLEASE DO NOT SLEEP IN THE ROAD.

The inns of Restwell are listed under glass on a barrel in the street, nineteen names, eleven of them crossed out and written in again. At the bottom, firmly: THE EIGHT HOURS IS THE ONE IN THE SONGS. THE OTHERS ARE ALSO VERY GOOD. Under that, in eighteen other hands, the word YES.

The others are indeed also very good. **The Seven Hours** is just as restful and one hour quicker, and has a clock an hour fast so that the guests feel rested sooner, and a card in the window saying BACK IN EIGHT HOURS. **The Nine Hours** is for those who find eight a little rushed. **The Four Hours** is for the busy hero, and is closed, gone to bed. **The Traveller's Rest**, **The Traveller's Second Rest** and **The Traveller's Rest (Annexe)** are all over the same door, and it is the only door there is. **The Lie-In** serves breakfast until four. **The Nightcap** is open all night for those who cannot sleep, and in Restwell that is nobody, and its landlord has never served a drink and is very well rested.

The town well has a sign, PLEASE DRAW WATER QUIETLY, PEOPLE ARE SLEEPING. A water barrel is labelled FOR WAKING GUESTS. USE SPARINGLY, and under that, in a different hand, USE NEVER. Pillows are ordered by the barrel. Spare nightcaps are sorted by inn, with a tassel in each inn's colours. At the edge of town is a log worn into a comfortable dip by travellers who could not quite make it as far as the inns, and slept there instead, very well.

A satisfied guest coming out of the street at four in the afternoon told the Forty-Eighth good morning, and that he had slept like the dead, eight hours, easy, best night in years. He had gone up at noon. He said it without the slightest difficulty, and walked off whistling eight bars of something.

## The Eight Hours

The Eight Hours is kept by **Mrs Hester Ashbury**. A room is ten crowns, breakfast included. Breakfast is whenever you come down; she argued about that for a year, and now she just cooks, and in Restwell breakfast is served after lunch and is therefore a chop. Asked about the songs, she says that resurrection is extra, and that she will want the room back by noon. It is generally already twenty past two.

Her husband, **Walt Ashbury**, sits on the bottom step of the stairs with a fiddle, where he can see the whole room, and plays every guest up: eight bars, the same eight bars, every time, for twenty-eight years now. It has to be that one. He tried a different one once, and they came down tired. Mrs Ashbury has listened to those eight bars for twenty-eight years and has never once asked him to stop. She says that he is very good at it.

The register is open on the bar, ruled in a firm hand. Every entry on every page says eight hours, every entry is in a different hand, and nobody has ever written anything else, except once, *eight hrs*, where somebody was in a hurry. When the Forty-Eighth came through, the last two entries read:

> Party of six. Up 12.02. Down 12.09. Slept: eight hours. Remarks: lovely.
>
> Mr & Mrs Tolley. Up 12.11. Down 12.19. Slept: eight hours. Remarks: LOVELY.

I print the register as it stands. The Tolleys are a young couple who were travelling that year, and they turn up elsewhere in this volume, at <a href="Hithermost">Hithermost</a>, in the Book, where they had just been married.

**Dr Pike** has a chair by the fire and a bottle labelled TONIC - DR PIKE, which is cold tea, and he has never once claimed otherwise. He is retired, in the practical sense, which is to say nobody comes. He took a room for a fortnight, thirteen years ago, to think about it. Twelve hundred guests a year go up the stairs, and every one of them comes down better, and nobody can tell him why. He watches. He looks at the fiddle player for slightly too long, and then back at his breakfast. He has theories. He does not like them.

Merribell has told me that cold tea is a perfectly good tonic, and that I am not to tell him she said so.

<div class="marginalia">I took a room at the Eight Hours on my way back from the coast road, and Walt played me up, and I came down, and it was morning, and I agreed that it was. I looked at the clock afterwards and I would rather not say. I have written in the register: eight hours. Remarks: I am a historian, and I should like to have stayed longer.</div>
