<p class="lede"><b>Upper Clanging</b> is a works town in the north-west, at the top of the west road, built on a hill round the sheds of the <a class="new" href="../Institutions/The_Hoyle_Works">Hoyle Works</a>, which have been trying to fly to the <a href="The_Obligatory_Tower">Obligatory Tower</a> for two hundred years. It is loud, early, grey and extremely proud of all three. In the year 2000 the two hundredth attempt got there.</p>

The town read the Prophecy two hundred years ago, believed every word of it, and made an engineering programme out of "every hundred years". It does not count. It has never needed to. Everybody up here is too busy.

## The street

**The sign** is a works hazard plate on a post, repainted: UPPER CLANGING. IT WILL BE QUIETER SOON. Further up, beside the parish rooms, there is a bench with a little plate, IN MEMORY OF THE QUIET, and somebody has scratched under it, IT WILL BE BACK SOON. Nobody in the town believes either of them, and both are kept painted.

**The clock** has been four minutes fast since 1841. The town sets its watches by it, and is therefore four minutes early for everything, and has been for a hundred and sixty years, and gets a great deal done. There is a card under the copy in the Safety Valve: THIS CLOCK IS RIGHT. EVERYWHERE ELSE IS LATE.

**The hooter** sounds at six, as usual, and at six on Sunday as well. The parish notices say that the parish has been asked about this, and that the parish has considered it. The minutes of the parish council have the hooter as item one, the hooter again as item two, and the hooter under any other business. It has its own book, on the shelf at the inn: *The Hooter: Its History, Its Uses, Its Critics.*

**The works** is at the top of the street, and has no sign. It has never needed one: it is where the noise is. There is an enamel plate beside the door, DANGER. HOT. LOUD. EARLY, and all four are true. There is a tank marked COAL OIL with NO WIZARDS WITHIN TEN YARDS chalked under it, which <a href="../People/Zephyrine_Vance">Zephyrine Vance</a> took personally, and says she was not wrong to. There is a skip with the boiler of Number 147 in it, and somebody has chalked on the boiler, very small: ALMOST.

**Smaller things worth stopping for.** A pump marked DRINKING WATER, and under it, SORT OF. An oil drum leaking into a gutter somebody dug for it, which runs to a bucket, which is full, and has a gutter of its own, to the next bucket. A front room in which every ornament on every shelf has been turned round to face the works, out of loyalty. Geraniums in a window box, grey like everything else in Clanging, and trying. A pillar box, collections at nine, twelve and five, with a little step built in front of the slot, worn smooth, for somebody quite small who posts a great many letters.

## The Safety Valve

The inn, kept by **Mr Kell**, is named for the one part of a boiler whose entire job is to give up before anything else does. They are very proud of it. A plate under the sign reads IN THE EVENT OF PRESSURE, GO TO BED, and a plate on the foot of each bed reads IN CASE OF EMERGENCY, LIE DOWN, which is the whole philosophy of the house on one plate. Beds are thirty crowns. You will be dry, and up here that is worth thirty.

On the wall is a framed list in a very good hand, *Things That Are Not To Happen Again*, numbered to sixty-three. I give a selection: 7. THE GEESE. 19. NOT LIKE THAT. 31. HOT. 44. MR PADGETT'S HAT (AND MR PADGETT). 58. ANY OF IT. 61. OLLERENSHAW. Number sixty-three is one very long word, and nobody in the room will say it aloud. There is a commentary on the list, much longer, which is not allowed out of the inn. I have not seen it.

On the dresser is the inn's best china, with a card on top, FOR WHEN SHE FLIES. It was dusted every week for as long as anybody can remember, and never used. I am told that it has now been used once, and dusted since, and put back.

**Winnie Marsden** sits by the fire with a half of shandy on a doily embroidered CLASS OF '19. The Class of 'Nineteen is the town's Cold Winter year: the foundry was banked down for six weeks that January, there was no work, there was no heat in the whole town but what was in the houses, and two hundred and forty children were born the following autumn, near enough all together. They have had a dinner every year since, on the second Saturday, with a seating plan settled when they were four. You cannot get in. You had to be born in it. Mrs Tunnicliffe at the parish rooms keeps the census, and was born in June, and is not invited. I put down here only what the census says, which is dates.

## The forge

**Ollerenshaw's** is at the foot of the street, under a painted sword and three lines of lettering: BRYD - SMITH; THE BEST IN THE NORTH; and, in a different paint, SAYS WHO. The third line is <a href="../People/Hob_Grumnir">Hob Grumnir</a>'s, from the year 2000. He asked me not to tell Bryd. Bryd knows.

**Bryd Ollerenshaw** has the forge from his father, who had it from his mother. If it is iron and it has to hold, it goes to him. Miss Hoyle sends him a drawing every spring, and every spring it is a spar. He and Hob were apprenticed together, and he has waited twenty years for Hob to admit that some jobs want two smiths. The meeting between them in the year 2000 consisted of each saying the other's surname and finding something wrong with the other's anvil, and was described to me by a child of the town as "what they do every time". They then made the Two Hundred's main spar in an afternoon without a word, and went down the hill to the house that does a pale, already arguing about who was buying. Half the town has money on which of them will admit the other is the better smith. Nobody has collected.

In the forge there is a certificate, BEST SMITH IN THE NORTH, awarded by B. Ollerenshaw to B. Ollerenshaw, in real gold leaf. There is a cabinet of three drawers: RIVETS; OTHER RIVETS; RIVETS (GRUMNIR'S - DO NOT USE). There is a quenching tub which hisses faintly whenever anybody in the room says *Thistlewick*. Merribell has told me that it is steam, and that I am not to encourage it.

## The parish rooms

The Parish Rooms take births, deaths and whatever else nobody else will take on. **Mrs Tunnicliffe** keeps the register, the census, the counter, the tea, the urn and the hymn board; the rota says so, and under it, EVERYTHING ELSE - SEE ABOVE. There are three tea urns, for the three sizes of parish emergency: a funeral, a wedding and the fete. Every one of those is on a Saturday.

The parish register lies open at OCCUPATION, and every line of it reads AT THE WORKS, page after page, in one hand, for eighty years. One entry, dated 1802, reads AT THE WORKS (THINKING). Somebody has pencilled HOYLE in the margin beside it and then rubbed it out, because the register does not take marginal notes.

The parish lost-and-found book has the entry FOUND: ONE BOY, NINE, WITH A PACKED BAG, ON THE SOUTH ROAD. RETURNED, and then FOUND: THE SAME. RETURNED, and it goes on like that for two pages.

## The Cotterills

The house at the end of the street is the Cotterills', and the street is mostly theirs as well. **Mrs Cotterill** has the names off by heart, because she chose them: Cotter, Rivet, Gudgeon, Tappet, Ferrule, Grommet, Clevis, Shim, and Spare, because they had run out, and Mr Cotterill said call it Spare, and she was tired. **Mr Cotterill** is on nights, twelve on and twelve off, and is always very glad to get home. He says that he has never once minded the walk.

The tenth, **Washer**, was born in the spring of 2000, at six in the morning, on the hooter. Mrs Cotterill says it is the only one of them that was ever on time. Mr Cotterill had said not Washer. Spare got to it first. Clevis was running a book on the name, and Washer came in at twenty to one, because half the works had a farthing on it once Mr Cotterill said not Washer, and Clevis had to pay out most of Upper Clanging. The book is closed. Mrs Cotterill says that there is not going to be another one.

I have the family's ages from Gudgeon, who copies the register and has written *Cotterill* more than four hundred times in the best hand Mrs Tunnicliffe has had: in this year of 2002 Cotter is twenty-six, Rivet twenty-four, Gudgeon twenty-two, Tappet twenty, Ferrule eighteen, Grommet seventeen, Clevis fifteen, Shim thirteen, Spare eleven, and Washer will be two this spring. Through the kitchen window there is a line of vests in order of size, and the peg on the end that was put there in hope has a vest on it now. Round the rim of the rain barrel there are ten tin cups on ten nails.

Cotter married **Bessie Marsden**, Winnie's granddaughter, and there was a cradle by their range in the year 2000 which nobody had mentioned and everybody had looked at. Hers came in the summer, as she said it would, on time. The Class of 'Nineteen's seating plan has a high chair in it now, which Winnie drew in herself, in pencil.

Spare's own article tells what Spare did. Here I will say only that there is a parish form, P-9, *Application by a Minor to Accompany a Chosen One*, which nobody had ever asked for, and that Gudgeon knew exactly which drawer it was in without looking.

<div class="marginalia">Mrs Cotterill reads Spare's letters out to the works at the dinner hour, all four lines of each, slowly, with the punctuation in. I asked whether she had kept them. She has kept all of them. She said that there was no dragon, and that she did more potatoes anyway.</div>
