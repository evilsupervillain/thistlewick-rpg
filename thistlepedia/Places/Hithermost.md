<p class="lede"><b>Hithermost</b> is a village at the foot of the mountain in the far north-west, the last before it and, for some, the first after it. People go into the mountain through a door at the top of the village, and most of them come back, and quite a few come back in through the porch of the chapel, looking puzzled, and the village gives them tea.</p>

## The village

The sign says HITHERMOST. THE LAST VILLAGE BEFORE THE MOUNTAIN, and under it, added later and in smaller letters, AND, FOR SOME, THE FIRST ONE AFTER IT. The well says LAST WATER BEFORE THE MOUNTAIN, and under it, FIRST WATER AFTER (SEE CHAPEL).

Asked what is after the mountain, a villager will say *Hithermost, mostly. They come back down, you see. We do teas.* The tea room's card says TEAS. HOT FOOD. KETTLE PERMITTING, and it is frequently not permitting, because the kettle is at the chapel again. Brother Quillon makes a great deal of tea, and most of it has been made in that kettle. The teapot stands, when it is in, on an iron trivet worked like a sprig of holly. It is Hob Grumnir's work. In the year 2000 he asked whether he might have it back, for a bit. The villager asked where they were to stand the pot, then; on the cloth? They come down off that mountain cold to the bone, every one of them, and the first thing they get is a pot of tea off that trivet. Hob said keep it.

The village has been saved a great deal. A house near the bottom answers a knock with *Not today, thank you*, in the tone of a house that has been saved four times this year and is still finding the bits, and you hear a cupboard being locked. Through its window a family sits in its front room, braced. A rain barrel has its lid weighted down with a very large stone and a card: THE BARREL HAS ALSO BEEN SAVED. PLEASE STOP. Another house has pots by the door, and under one of them a note: THERE IS NO KEY UNDER ANY OF THE POTS. PLEASE PUT THE POTS BACK. Under another: STILL NO.

There is a mountaineer, whose door is locked with a card on the knocker, GONE UP THE MOUNTAIN. BACK SHORTLY, and eleven more cards pinned under it in the same hand, all saying the same thing. He always does come back. Usually in through the chapel porch, looking puzzled. His calendar has GONE UP written on every Tuesday.

## The Chapel of Continuance

The board outside reads: CHAPEL OF CONTINUANCE. KEEPER OF THE RECORD: BR. QUILLON. SERVICES: CONTINUOUS. Travellers are asked to sign the Book before proceeding into the mountain, and again on the way back, if applicable, and again after that.

The glass is worth looking at. In one panel a saint goes into a mountain; in the next, the same saint comes out of a chapel porch, looking puzzled. In another a saint writes her name in a book, and again, and again, smaller, to make it fit. In another a saint with a candle stands at the door of a mountain, looking back over her shoulder, the way people do when they have done this before. And in the last a monk stands at a lectern with a cup of tea, and a very expressive silence, and the glazier has managed to get the silence in. You can see it.

**The Book** lies open on the lectern and glows, softly and evenly, with nothing to account for it. Those going into the mountain write their names in it, in case of misfortune. In case of misfortune they come back in through the porch, and write their names again. The page is in a great many hands. Most of them are the same hand. The old volumes are stacked beside the lectern, every one of them full, and the spine of each says CONTINUED. The porch has a plant in it which has been knocked over and stood up again so many times that it has grown a little to one side, to be ready.

**Brother Quillon** keeps the Record. Asked whether he remembers the people who sign it, he says that the Order forbids it, and that it is the only thing the Order forbids. He smiles, serenely, with his eyes shut. It is a very expressive smile. It has had forty years. He says that every soul deserves a second chance, and does not look at Sir Dunstable, very clearly indeed, and says something under his breath that sounds like a number in the low two hundreds.

Every afternoon at ten past four he pours a cup of tea and puts it on the lectern. It is still warm. It is always still warm.

## Sir Dunstable

<div class="disputed">

**Sir Dunstable of the Vale** is a knight in armour that has been polished a great many times and scorched very slightly more, who comes in through the chapel porch at a quarter past four, every day, to the minute, and signs the Book, just in case, and finds his tea waiting, and is delighted. "Oh, how kind. Every time! The fellow's a mind-reader." Brother Quillon says nothing, with enormous tact. Sir Dunstable explains, with some warmth, that nothing happened. He has been through the Door once or twice. Went in, had a look round, came out. Grand day.

There are, in the village and well beyond it, **competing theories** about Sir Dunstable, and I set out the evidence on both sides as fairly as I am able. I have not decided between them, and I am not going to.

*That he is alive.* He takes the tea, and is delighted by it, every time. He borrowed the village's lamp ("Back directly. - D."), and after the Quest he lent it to the Forty-Eighth, in person, handle first, with lovely manners, and it was a perfectly solid lamp, and the Forty-Eighth carried it down the Stair and has it still. He is a shareholder in the Kingdom of <a href="Fairholme">Fairholme</a>, with a chair and a place card, and the council minutes list him under *apologies* (in a mountain), not under deceased, and the council minutes everything. He is very slightly more singed each time, and the singeing is cumulative, which is a living thing's habit. And the Forty-Eighth's party came back in through the porch too, and nobody suggests that the Forty-Eighth is dead.

*That he is not.* The Book is his name all the way down the page, *Just in case*, and Brother Quillon's number is in the low two hundreds. He comes in through the porch, the way people come back who went through the Door and did not come out of it. His chalk marks down the Stair, each a D. and a number and each a little deeper than the last, stop at D. 212, and nobody had been past it. Something on the Stair is always on its way up, in the dark, and nobody has seen who. And a quarter past four, every day, to the minute.

The village has its own evidence, and every piece of it has an answer. His laundry is done on Mondays by the woman at the tea room, who says that it is always a little singed, and always dry, and that she has never once seen him bring it or fetch it; it is simply there in the basket at a quarter past four. He receives post: the Fairholme minutes, every quarter, and a seed catalogue. He has never been seen to read either, and both are gone by the morning. Whether he casts a shadow by lamplight has been argued in the tea room for years, and is not settled, because the lamp he would cast it by is the village's, and he has had it, and lately the Forty-Eighth has had it. The tea does not go cold. Brother Quillon will not say whether that is the tea.

I asked Sir Dunstable directly, on my visit, in the chapel, at a quarter past four. He said that it was very good of me to come so far, and that nothing had happened, and signed the Book. I asked why he signed it every time. He said: "Just in case."

My own view is that the knight is a man of excellent manners, and that the question is none of my business.

At the Slain Wyvern there is a book on it. It is held by Sops. The odds have not moved in two years, which Sops says has never happened before on anything, and which he declines to interpret.

</div>

## The Door into the Mountain

At the top of the village, a timbered mouth in the rock, and past it a stair going down into a dark that does not stop. A board is nailed to the frame: NO ADMITTANCE WITHOUT A LAMP. BY ORDER. LAMP ON HOOK. There is a hook under the board, and no lamp on it, and chalked beside it, in a cheerful hand, *Borrowed lamp. Back directly. - D.*

Until the Quest was over the Forty-Eighth had no lamp, and did not go in. Sir Dunstable would not have dreamt of lending it before. A man with an appointment. Not done. Afterwards he had a free afternoon, and Sir Dunstable said that he would want the lamp, then, and unhooked it from his belt, and asked for it back directly, because he goes in at a quarter past four. He did not say which quarter past four. He has not asked for it back. See <a href="The_Stair">The Stair</a>.
