<p class="lede">The <b>Quills</b> are the family of <a href="../Places/Thistlewick">Thistlewick</a> that writes the official account of each Chosen One for the <a href="The_Prophecy_Committee">Prophecy Committee</a>, in metre, and has done so for four generations. They are very good at it. Until the year 2000 none of them had ever written down what actually happened, and none of them had needed to, because the shape is known.</p>

## The office

When the Committee chooses, a Quill begins. The account is begun the morning the Committee votes, and is approved on the Tuesday, and is very often finished before the Chosen One is, because a Chosen One has a beginning and an end, and in between he walks, and the walking can be written ahead. The Committee approves every account. It does not read them. The Quills have known this for a long time, and have gone on writing them well, which I think is the most professional thing in Thistlewick.

The Quills also write the text under the portraits on the Wall of the Forty-Seven, every one of them, and their cards are tucked into the frames. The Prophecy's own preamble quotes one of them, Piper's grandmother, who wrote numbers forty to forty-three.

I have noticed, being a <a href="../Places/Nether_Sopping">Nether Sopping</a> man, that four generations is a short time in which to write forty-seven accounts that are a hundred years apart. Nobody in the family has remarked on this, and I have not raised it with them, because the answer is in clause two, and in <a href="../History/Two_Reckonings">Two Reckonings</a>, and because it is not the Quills' fault what the Committee finds convenient.

## The house style

The house style is one line long: *never let the truth spoil the metre*. It is not cynical. It is a craftsman's rule, and it is kept by craftsmen. It produces accounts that scan, which is more than most history does; I have read Volume Two again, and I know.

Its conventions are easily learned, and every account keeps them. The Chosen One is an orphan of humble birth, because nobody goes north with a mother; it does not scan. One of his companions dies, in about canto nine, of nothing in particular, because it scans. He slays the <a href="../People/Grimspite_the_Inevitable">Dark Lord</a> at the top of the <a href="../Places/The_Obligatory_Tower">Tower</a> in a thunderstorm, in a rhyme on *tower* that was never going to be anything but *hour*. And there is a wolf. Piper's grandmother had a wolf in forty-one, forty-two and forty-three, and it was the same wolf. A good wolf.

## The rebellion

Piper Quill's whole professional rebellion has been to let the truth spoil the metre. She narrates things as they happen, in the third person, at volume, whether or not it is convenient, and writes them down, and some of them scan. She walked north with the Forty-Eighth to do it, which no Quill had done before, the shape being known.

On the way she read every verse in the Committee's box of struck material, on Form S-7, and printed none; finished the ballad; and came second at the Bell in <a href="../Places/Great_Dunnage">Great Dunnage</a>, by one point, to a man who does birdcalls. She is, as her family has noticed, the first Quill ever to win a prize for writing down what actually happened, and it was for coming second, and she says that is about right.

## The account of the Forty-Eighth

The official account of the Forty-Eighth is <i>The Lay of the Forty-Eighth</i>, by A. Quill, in twelve cantos, with a very good middle. What it says now is a matter on which the Quills disagree with one another, very civilly, and have asked me not to settle. The card in the forty-eighth frame on the Wall gives the account, and who wrote it, and a stamp; I have seen the card, and I say only that there are, depending on whom you ask, one official account of the Forty-Eighth or two, and that both are the authorised version. The articles on <a href="../People/Ambrosine_Quill">Ambrosine Quill</a> and <a href="../People/Piper_Quill">Piper Quill</a> give both, and so does the Committee's own Clerk, who has been heard to say either APPROVED or AMENDED, depending on the day, and looking pleased about it either way.

What is not in dispute is that, for the first time in four generations, somebody told a Quill that her account was very good, and meant it, and that she has not forgotten it.

## Afterwards

There is no Forty-Ninth to write. The Quills are Chroniclers to the Committee still, and the Committee still meets, and the Quills still attend, and take the minutes in metre when the Clerk is not looking. Ambrosine reads her own account again most mornings, she says, for the middle. Piper is writing everything else down, and her notebooks are in double figures.

The two of them nod to one another now, across a room, as one professional to another. I asked each of them, separately, which of them was the better writer. Ambrosine said Piper. Piper said Ambrosine. Then they both said that I was not, and that it was not close, and I have decided to take that as a family resemblance.
