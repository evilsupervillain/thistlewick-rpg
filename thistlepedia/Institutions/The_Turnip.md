<p class="lede">The <b>turnip</b> is a root vegetable, and is grown, eaten, painted, sat on, sung about, pressed between the pages of souvenir books, carved into the shape of a sword for an occasion, kept back for a supper that is a hundred years late, and taken very seriously indeed in <a href="../Places/Thistlewick">Thistlewick</a>, which grows nothing else, and has never seen the need. It is the produce of the village, the charge on its crest, the name of its inn, the subject of its only song and the profession of its most famous son. Thistlewick asked that it be filed in this volume under Institutions. I have done so. I would like it understood that I did not argue, and that this was not because I agreed.</p>

## A note on the filing

I define an institution, in the introduction to this volume, as a group of people who have agreed to keep minutes. The turnip keeps no minutes. When I put this to the parish of Thistlewick, by letter, I received in reply a letter of four pages from the clerk to the Prophecy Committee, setting out that the turnip had been in the village longer than the Committee had, that it had sent forty-eight Chosen Ones north on its strength, that it was on the crest and over the door of the inn, and that if those were not the marks of an institution then the clerk would be glad to know what were. The fourth page was about biscuits, and I think was from another letter.

<a href="../People/Wren_Halloway">Wren Halloway</a>, who keeps the Bestiary in this volume and who does not care for any of this, wrote to say that the turnip is a plant, *Brassica rapa*, and that she has already given it an entry, under its militant form, and that if it went anywhere else it should go in the Bestiary, and that nobody should send her theories. I have not sent her a theory. I have sent her this article, which is not a theory, but is long, and she has written back one word, which was *fine*, and was underlined, which I have chosen to read as approval.

<div class="marginalia">A vegetable, then, filed under the institutions, between a college that has been referring things since about 1400 and a works that has been building airships for two hundred years behind schedule. I have looked at it there for some time, in proof, and I think it holds its own. - H.B.</div>

## What a turnip is

I had better say at the start what a turnip is, because I am a Nether Sopping man and we do not grow them, and so I have had to find out, and it has taken me the best part of two years, and I am still not certain I have it all.

A turnip is a root. It is round, or roundish, white at the bottom and purple at the top, where it has seen the sun, and it has leaves, which Thistlewick does not eat, except in a hard winter, and does not talk about afterwards. It grows in the ground. It is planted in the spring and pulled in the autumn, and between the two it does nothing at all that anybody can see, which I am told is the whole art of it, and which I am told also by the farmers of Thistlewick is a great deal harder than it looks, and I believe them, because they say it with the face of people who have tried to explain it before.

I asked a farmer of Thistlewick what made a good turnip, and he thought about it for a long time, leaning on a gate, and then said, "Being a turnip." I asked another, and she said, "Not being a parsnip," and looked at me as if I might be one. I asked <a href="../People/Bram_Thistle">Bram Thistle</a>, who is the authority, and he said that a good turnip was one that came up where you put it, and was a turnip when it did, and that you would be surprised how often that was all anybody needed, and that he did not only mean turnips. I have thought about that a good deal since. I think it is the best definition in this volume, and I have looked.

The Bestiary gives the turnip one line of description, which is that it is "a turnip, in every particular but one". Thistlewick gives it no description at all. Asked to describe one, a Thistlewick person will hold one up.

## The fields

The turnip fields of Thistlewick go round the village on three sides. The fourth side is the pond, which has no fish in it, and would, I suspect, have been turnips also if anybody had been able to work out how to drain it. The village fingerpost on the square reads:

<div class="stamp">NORTH: The Obligatory Tower. 200 leagues.<br>
SOUTH: The turnip fields. 40 paces.<br>
EAST: The pond. There are no fish in it.<br>
WEST: Also the turnip fields.</div>

It is the most accurate signpost I have ever read, and I have read a great many, some of them in Firstfield, which explain themselves at length. It does not mention that the fields to the south and the fields to the west are the same fields, going round the corner, because to a Thistlewick person this does not need mentioning. To a visitor it is the first thing you notice, and then the only thing.

Several of the rows are worth seeing, and I give them in order, walking down from the lane.

**The Thistle row.** Bram Thistle's own row, planted the spring of the year 2000, a few weeks before he was Chosen. It came up beautifully, he says, "which felt a little pointed, at the time". It has a neat hole in it with a label on a stick, which is the type locality of a species of science, and is under <a href="../Bestiary/Bramus_agricola">Bramus agricola</a>. The species eats turnip. Its namesake does too. Neither of them has been asked to comment on this and I am not going to be the one.

**The flag.** In the next row but one is a turnip with a tiny flag stuck in it, very old, reading KEPT BACK FOR THE CHOSEN ONE'S SUPPER. Nobody knows which Chosen One. The flag is older than anybody's grandmother's grandmother, and they plant a fresh turnip under it every spring, just in case, and pull the old one out in the autumn and eat it, which I think is the most sensible thing Thistlewick does, and it has, I am told, been very good for the turnips. I asked whether the Forty-Eighth had been given the turnip, when he came home, it being presumably his. I was told that he had not asked. I asked him whether he wanted it, and he said it was the Chosen One's, and he was not sure he was, now, particularly, and that whoever was going to have it should have it fresh. It is still there. There is still a new one every spring.

**The sign.** The row after that has a small and tired sign:

<div class="stamp">PLEASE DO NOT PULL THE TURNIPS UP TO SEE IF ONE OF THEM IS A SWORD IN A STONE.<br>
NONE OF THEM IS. WE HAVE CHECKED. ALL OF THEM.</div>

It is the only sign in Thistlewick in the past tense, and the farmer whose row it is says that it went up after the twenty-third Chosen One, or thereabouts, and has been needed by every one since, and by a great many people who were not Chosen at all, but had hopes. It is a very tired sign. I believe every word on it.

**The string.** The last row before the lane is so straight that somebody must have used string. Somebody did. The string is still there. It is the Farmer's best string, and he would like it back after the harvest, and he has said this every year that I have been visiting, and the string is still there, and I have begun to suspect that he would not like it back after all, and only likes to say so.

**The scarecrow** wears Bram's old coat, with a note pinned to it: CHOSEN ONE (RESERVE). It has not been called on. It stands among the turnips facing north, and I do not think it is my place to say what it is thinking.

## The crest

Over the door of <a href="../Places/Prophecy_Hall">Prophecy Hall</a> is the arms of Thistlewick: **a turnip rampant, on a field of slightly different turnips.** The motto beneath is in Old Thistlewick, NON NOBIS, NISI FORTE: *not us, unless it has to be*.

A turnip rampant is a turnip standing on its hind leg, with its fore-leaves raised, which no turnip in nature has ever done, and the herald who drew it, whoever that was, had clearly never seen one rampant either, and got round the difficulty by drawing a turnip and leaning it slightly. It has been repainted so often that it is now mostly paint, and with every coat it has become a little more rampant, so that by now it is leaning very nearly at the angle of a man going up a hill into the wind. In another hundred years, I estimate, it will be lying down.

The field of slightly different turnips has given me more trouble than anything else in this article. I have asked three people in Thistlewick what the difference is, and have been told:

1. that the turnips of the field are *ordinary* turnips, and the turnip rampant is the turnip of Thistlewick, which is not ordinary, and that I should be able to see this;
2. that the turnips of the field are *last year's*;
3. that there is no difference, and there never was, and the word "slightly" was put in by the Committee to make a motion pass.

I have looked very hard at the crest for some time and I cannot see a difference either. I am inclined to the third account, which is the most like the Committee. I am also inclined to the first, because the woman who gave it to me had not the slightest doubt about it, and I have seldom been looked at with such pity.

The motto is the best thing about the arms, and Thistlewick does not seem to know it. *Not us, unless it has to be.* It is a very modest motto for a village that sends a hero north every hundred years. I put this to a member of the Committee, who said that the motto was about the turnips.

## At table

### Breakfast

In Thistlewick breakfast is turnip. It is turnip at the Gilded Turnip, where Prudence Thrupp's bed is twenty crowns and includes breakfast and breakfast is turnip, and she has never been asked to change this and would not, and it is turnip in the houses, and it was turnip on the morning the Forty-Eighth was Chosen, when his mother did it for him as a steak, for the occasion, and cut his into the shape of a sword. He has told me that it was the most supportive thing anybody did for him all day, and that he did not eat it at the time, and ate it later, as a sort of rehearsal.

I have stayed a night at the Gilded Turnip, and breakfast was turnip. It was a very good turnip. I would like that on the record, in case it is thought that a Nether Sopping man came to Thistlewick and was rude about the breakfast.

### The four kinds

The Thistles' pantry has jars and preserves and four kinds of turnip, and I asked <a href="../People/Mrs_Thistle">Mrs Thistle</a> what they were, and she told me, ticking them off on her fingers:

1. turnip;
2. turnip, mashed;
3. turnip, for visitors;
4. the other one.

I asked what the other one was. She said it was for when the first three would not do. I asked when that had been. She said, "Not yet." I have thought since that this is the turnip equivalent of the sword above the grate, and I said so, and she said that the sword had been a good deal more trouble.

The turnip for visitors is the same as the first kind, on a better plate.

### The cellar

The Gilded Turnip keeps turnip on its shelves in sacks and boxes, labelled by year like wine, and it has opinions about the years. **The '87** is said to be very good, and is not for sale, and is brought down for weddings and shown to the couple and taken back up. **The '91** is not spoken of. I tried. I asked Prudence, who looked at me; and the Regular, who looked at his drink; and a farmer, who looked at the '91 on the shelf, and then at me, and then said that it had been a wet year, and that was all he would say, and he would thank me not to bring it up in front of the sacks.

<div class="marginalia">I have tasted the '87. It tasted of turnip. I said so, and was told that this was the point, and that the '91 had not, and that this was the point also. - H.B.</div>

### Cordial, and further afield

Prudence makes a cordial of elderflower and turnip, which is better than it sounds, and would more or less have to be. The Field Tonic that is sold to adventurers in the south, which restores a whole company at once, also tastes of turnip, and nobody in the trade has been able to tell me why, and I have stopped asking, because the last man I asked said that most things did if you were from Thistlewick, and looked at me as though I might have been.

The market stall on the square at Thistlewick sells turnips, for two crowns, under a card that reads FRESH! MOSTLY! A turnip eaten in the field restores the eater by an amount which the Adventurers' Guild has measured and which I will not print, because it is very small, and the turnip is not to blame for that, and would be hurt. The Guild's own description of the article reads in full: *A turnip. Bram grew it and would prefer you did not throw it away.* I asked the Guild who had written that, and they said it had come with the turnip.

## The chair

The most famous turnip in the history of Thistlewick is not the one on the plinth. It is the one in the Thistles' front room.

Bram's great-grandmother, **Mabel Thistle**, 1890 to 1979, grew a turnip as big as a chair, and sat on it, and then it was the chair. Her headstone in the churchyard says so, in capitals: SHE GREW A TURNIP AS BIG AS A CHAIR, AND SAT ON IT, AND THEN IT WAS THE CHAIR. It is the only headstone I know of that records an item of furniture, and the family speaks of it in the tone other families keep for a war.

The chair is still in the family. Mrs Thistle uses it for the good visitors. I have sat on it. I would like to say two things about it, as its first historian.

The first is that it is very comfortable. It has been upholstered at some point, in green, and the upholstery has worn through at the arms, and underneath, where it has worn, it is turnip.

The second is that it ought not to be there. A turnip of the 1910s or thereabouts, which is my guess at the year, and Mrs Thistle's too, has no business being anything at all in 2002, let alone a chair. I put this to Mrs Thistle as delicately as I could, which was not very. She said that it had been well brought up.

I have no better explanation and I am not looking for one. I have sat in a great many chairs in the course of this history, some of them thrones, and one of them a duck's, and the Thistles' turnip is the one I would go back for.

## The show

Thistlewick has held a turnip show at the end of every harvest for longer than the Committee has minutes, in the yard behind Prophecy Hall, on trestles. There are classes for the heaviest, the roundest, the most even in colour and the most like a turnip, and a class for children which is for the turnip that looks most like somebody on the Committee, and is always the best-attended, and has caused two resignations.

The show is remembered for one woman. **Elspeth Moore**, 1902 to 1994, won it forty-one years running. Her stone in the churchyard says so, and under that, in smaller and much angrier capitals: THE FORTY-SECOND WAS FIXED.

I have tried to find out who won the forty-second. Nobody will tell me, which in Thistlewick is unusual, because everybody in Thistlewick will tell you everything, at length, at the gate. The minutes of the Committee for that year record that the show was held and was a credit to the parish, and the rest of the minute is about the biscuits. I asked whether it had been fixed, and was told that Elspeth had always said so, and that it was not for anybody now to say she was wrong, and that she had been very good with a turnip, and that this was more important. I agree.

There is a rosette on her stone every autumn that says SECOND. I had thought this was a joke against her, and was ready to be cross about it in print, and asked. It is put there every year, the morning after the show, by whoever has won it, and it is the first thing they do. It has been every winner since 1994, and none of them has ever discussed it with any of the others. I have stood in front of it, in the autumn, with my hat off, and I would do so again.

<div class="marginalia">Bram Thistle's crop of 2001, his first since the Quest, was the best in the parish, and the Committee asked whether it had been prophesied, and it had not. He did not enter it in the show. He says it was harvest, and he was busy. I think he did not want to have to put a rosette on Elspeth's stone, not because he minded doing it, but because he would have had to stand there in front of the whole village while he did. - H.B.</div>

## The turnip and the Prophecy

I come now to the part of this article that I expect to be read most closely, by the Committee, with a pencil.

The <a href="../History/The_Prophecy_of_Thistlewick">Prophecy of Thistlewick</a> does not mention the turnip in any of its clauses. I have read them all, more times than anybody who is not a clerk ought to, and it is not there. And yet every Chosen One that Thistlewick has ever sent has gone north from among the turnips, and the village has never seen this as a coincidence, and has treated the turnip throughout as something between a mascot and a guarantor.

**The flowers.** The Committee keeps a souvenir book, *The Forty-Seven: A Souvenir*, with a portrait, a date and a pressed flower on every page. The flowers are all turnip flowers. A turnip flower is small and yellow and nobody has ever looked at one on purpose, and there are forty-seven of them, pressed, each between two pages and each a little browner than the last, and I have seen the book, across a table, and was not allowed to touch it, and I think it is the most moving thing in Prophecy Hall, and I do not think the Committee knows that.

**The reference.** When the Forty-Eighth went south to the Adventurers' Guild at Nether Sopping, which would not register him without a character reference, Elder <a href="../People/Elder_Wispel">Wispel</a> wrote him one, with the good pen. It confirms that Bram Thistle is the Chosen One, is of good character, and grows a decent turnip. The last part is underlined. I asked the Elder why, and he said that the first two parts were the Committee's opinion, and the third was a matter of record.

**The portrait.** Over the fireplace in the Thistles' house hangs a painting of the family: six generations of Thistles, all holding turnips. Not one of them is holding a sword. Bram has told me that he looked at it on the morning he was Chosen and thought that he was going to be the odd one out. He is not, as it turned out. He came home and put the sword back, and he has a turnip.

**The road.** He took turnips with him. His mother's pantry gave him some on the morning he left, because they were his, and he ate them on the road, and the account of the Quest that is kept in Prophecy Hall, *as amended*, records the number, as it records everything else: who came back, how many of the well-worn conventions of adventuring were walked into, and how many turnips were eaten. I have the figure. He has asked me not to print it, on the grounds that it is nobody's business, and I agree, and I will only say that it is a real number, and that it is the right one.

<a href="../People/Mother_Selvidge">Mother Selvidge</a>, who reads fortunes in a striped booth at the back of a fair in the far south, read his, and told him that he would go a long way, and meet a great many people, and that at the end of it somebody would be very surprised to see him; "and you'll eat a turnip," she said. "That one's free." He did. On the way home. I have checked.

**The top.** At the top of the <a href="../Places/The_Obligatory_Tower">Obligatory Tower</a>, when it was all over, <a href="../People/Grimspite_the_Inevitable">Grimspite the Inevitable</a> signed him a receipt, and sent him home with a message:

> "Go home, forty-eight. Grow your turnips. There isn't a forty-nine. Say that to them, when you get in."

I have considered this sentence at length. It is the only instruction about the turnip ever given by a Dark Lord, so far as the record goes, and it was obeyed. *The next morning there were still turnips.* That is the first line of the history of Thistlewick after the Prophecy, and it is also, I think, the whole of it.

<div class="marginalia">The Committee has asked me to add that the turnip is not a party to the Prophecy, was never a party to the Prophecy, and is not in any way responsible for clause twelve. I add it. Nobody had suggested otherwise. - H.B.</div>

## The turnip in the arts

### Music

Thistlewick has one song, and it is *Turnips in the Morning*. It is played on the piano in the Gilded Turnip, which has a sign on the lid saying PLEASE DO NOT PLAY THE PIANO, and under that, smaller, THIS MEANS YOU, BRAM, and he played it anyway, with one finger, on the morning he left, it being the one tune he knew, and the whole inn went quiet and then went back to being loud, a little faster than before.

The tin tankard that hangs on a bracket over the inn door creaks when the wind blows, in exactly the rhythm of *Turnips in the Morning*, and has taught it to three generations of the village. I do not know whether the tankard learned it from the village or the village from the tankard. Nobody in Thistlewick finds this an interesting question.

It was thought for a long time to be a bad tune. It is not. It was played, in the year 2000, properly, by <a href="../People/Piper_Quill">Piper Quill</a>, who is a professional, on the same piano, and it turned out that it had been a good tune all along, and had been waiting for somebody to use more than one finger. The village has not got over this. Some of them, I am told, are rather annoyed.

The words, which I was sung at the Turnip by four regulars at once, I will not give here, because there are eleven verses, and the first ten are about turnips, and the eleventh is about turnips also, but in the morning.

### Verse

There is no rhyme in the language for *turnip*. This is well known now, and it is well known because the whole of the Forty-Eighth's company sat down one afternoon in the year 2000 in front of the gilded turnip on its plinth and tried to find one for Piper Quill, who had needed one since the night she joined, for the last verse of her ballad, and had said she would get one on the road, and had not.

The afternoon is in her notebook, <a href="../People/From_the_Notebooks_of_P._Quill#a-rhyme-for-turnip">which is printed in this volume</a>, and I will not give all of it, because everybody I asked says that they were there, and that their suggestion was the best, and some of them were not there, and I am not going to adjudicate between a blacksmith and a knight about a rhyme. I give the ones I have been able to confirm, in the order of the notebook, which is the order of how bad they were:

* *Burnip*. From the blacksmith, who said that it was a word now, and that he had made worse out of less.
* Nothing ending in *-nip*. From the naturalist, pre-emptively, on the grounds that a nip is a bite, or a pinch, or catmint, genus *Nepeta*, and is inadmissible.
* *Navet*, which is turnip in the old tongue of Sir Aldric's house, and rhymes very beautifully with nothing, in that tongue either.
* *Turnip*. Located, on behalf of a client, by <a href="../People/Nix">Nix</a>, written on a folded slip, with the provenance on the back. The client had not said it had to be a different word.
* *Sherbet*. A slant rhyme, defended by the hedge mage on the grounds that the Collegium would accept it, which I am told is not, in itself, a defence.
* Something anatomical. From the field medic, who began at the scalp and went down, aloud, in order, and the Gilded Turnip went quiet somewhere around the pelvis, and she went right down to the feet, and there was nothing.
* *Parsnip*. From the Forty-Eighth, who pointed out that it was very nearly there. It is a different vegetable.

The answer, in the end, was that there is no rhyme for turnip and there does not need to be, and Piper Quill moved the turnip down a line and ended the other line on *grate*, which has several, and it took nine seconds, and everybody who had spent the afternoon on it was furious:

<div class="verse">
<p>'And so he came home to the fields,<br>
and hung his sword above the grate,<br>
and sat down with a turnip, which<br>
was worth the journey, and the wait.'</p>
<span class="attribution">P. Quill, the ballad, last verse</span>
</div>

It is the last verse. The turnip is in the middle of a line, where nothing has to rhyme with it, and I have come to think that that is where a turnip is happiest.

Her mother's *Lay of the Forty-Eighth*, which the Committee approved on a Tuesday, is in twelve cantos, and there is no turnip in any of them. The Forty-Eighth read all twelve again, looking for one. I have read them too. It is very good. There is not a turnip in it anywhere, and I think it is the worse for it, and I have said so to <a href="../People/Ambrosine_Quill">Mrs Quill</a>, who said that a Lay is not the place for a vegetable, and I said that it depended on the hero.

## The turnip abroad

Outside Thistlewick the turnip is not well understood, and the turnip farmer less so, and the Forty-Eighth found, on the road, that saying what he did for a living had a curious effect on people with clipboards. I set some of them down.

At the Chapter House of the <a href="The_Order_of_the_Errant">Order of the Errant</a>, at <a href="../Places/Coldbarrow">Coldbarrow</a>, he was asked whether he was a knight, and said that he grew turnips, and the clerk said that in that case he was not on the map, and seemed very relieved.

At the Smiths' Hall in <a href="../Places/Great_Dunnage">Great Dunnage</a> he was asked whether he was a smith, and said that he was a turnip farmer, and was told that in that case he might look, and was not to touch.

At the edge of <a href="../Places/Wayside_Sights#the-cinder-fen">the Cinder Fen</a> he was asked by <a href="../People/The_Drainage_Commissioner">the Drainage Commissioner</a> whether he was a fire person, and said that he was a turnip person, and she explained that the Fen wanted burning, nine hundred acres of it, and did not seem to feel that his answer had made any difference.

In <a href="../Places/Fairholme">Fairholme</a>, where the kingdom has been divided among everybody who ever rescued its princess, one of the shareholders told him that she had got a turnip field and the north bridge, and that the bridge was the troll's, and they took turns. I have been to see the field. It is not as good as Thistlewick's. She knows.

At <a href="../Places/Nether_Sopping">Nether Sopping</a>, in the outfitters, there is a crate marked RETURNS, full of compasses, and every one of them is pointing at the Gilded Turnip in Thistlewick. Mrs Barrow says it is a known fault. I do not dispute it. I have only to say that I have held one of those compasses in my hand, in Nether Sopping, forty miles away, and turned round with it, slowly, and it stayed pointing at Thistlewick the whole time, and at the turnip, and I put it down rather quickly.

<div class="marginalia">A guess, and marked as one. Thistlewick has believed for as long as it has had a fingerpost that it is the middle of the world. I do not believe this. I am a Nether Sopping man. But I have seen the compasses, and I am not, after that, as certain as I was. - H.B.</div>

And at Number Forty-Five's cottage, in Nether Sopping, there is not one turnip. <a href="../People/Halbert_Quy">Halbert Quy</a>, the Forty-Fifth, who came home from the Tower in 1970 having been offered a cup of tea at the top and said no, grows roses, and has crates of seed labelled in a careful hand, SEEDS, MORE SEEDS, SEEDS (ROSES), and SEEDS (NOT TURNIPS), and has had no turnip in the house for thirty years, as a policy. He told me in 2001 that the policy had outlived its purpose, and that he was keeping it up out of loyalty, and I asked him to whom, and he said that he was not entirely sure, and that it was a nice policy, and that the roses liked it.

## The creatures of the turnip

The turnip is host to at least three kinds of creature, by the Bestiary's count, and the Bestiary would like it known that it does not count the Committee.

**The Militant Turnip.** In the fields about Thistlewick, on the road, in company with crows, there are turnips that have had enough. Wren Halloway has examined one closely. Its anatomy is a turnip's and its conduct is not. The <a href="../Bestiary/The_Road_North#turnip">Bestiary</a> has it, and the Forty-Eighth, who takes these personally, has told me that he was not in the least surprised by them, and that if I had grown them for twenty years I would not be either. Thistlewick does not acknowledge the Militant Turnip at all. Asked about it, the village will say that those are not Thistlewick turnips, and that they must have come from somewhere else, and look south, at Nether Sopping, where we do not grow turnips, which I have pointed out, and which they say proves it.

**The turnip gnome.** A gnome that lives in turnips, small, brown, bearded and of fixed opinions; the <a href="../Bestiary/The_Fauna_of_the_Valley#turnip-gnome">Bestiary</a> gives his name as *Mrs Rowbotham*, after the wife of the man who named him. She knew.

***Bramus agricola*** (Halloway, 2000). A small brown burrowing animal of turnip fields, which eats turnip, survives things it has no business surviving, and is of no consequence whatsoever. It was named in the year 2000 after the Forty-Eighth, at a burrow in his own row, by the naturalist of his company, who had him sit down on a turnip while she explained it. <a href="../Bestiary/Bramus_agricola">Its article</a> is the most affectionate thing in the Bestiary, and I do not think its author would like me to say so, and I have said so.

## The gilded one

The most looked-at turnip in the world is a single turnip on a plinth in an alcove of the <a href="../Places/The_Gilded_Turnip">Gilded Turnip</a>, painted gold, under a small plaque that reads THE GILDED TURNIP. It is not a metaphor. It is just a turnip somebody painted. It gives the inn its name, and the inn gives the turnip its reputation, and neither will say which came first.

I asked how long it had been there. I was told that it had been there since before Prudence's father, and that it had been repainted every so often, and that nobody had ever looked at whether it was the same turnip under the paint, and that nobody was going to, and that it did not matter, and that I was not to touch it.

Piper Quill called it "the problem", and she meant the rhyme. I have come round to thinking that she was right in a larger way than she meant. It is a turnip that somebody painted gold, because they wanted something in the room to be grand, and it is still very obviously a turnip, and everybody in the room knows it, and everybody in the room loves it. If you wanted a picture of Thistlewick you could do a great deal worse. The Committee has, and has it on the wall in Prophecy Hall, forty-seven times.

## Since

Since the year 2000 the turnip has gone on very much as before, which Thistlewick regards as the victory.

The Forty-Eighth farms the Thistle ground with his mother, as he did before he was Chosen, and his crop of 2001 was the best in the parish. He declined a seat on the Prophecy Committee, which was offered, because it meets in planting time. He keeps *The Turnip Grower's Almanac* for 1997 by his bed, and he tells me that it is still right about everything but the weather, and was never right about that.

There is a turnip in a pot on the sill of the Thistles' front window, beside his mother's geraniums, which he is growing as an experiment. It has not done anything you would call an experiment. He has told me what the experiment is, and I have promised not to say, and I will say only that it is about whether a turnip minds where it is, and that so far it does not seem to.

On the sill of Prophecy Hall's other window the village school has made a row of little clay figures, one for each Chosen One, and the forty-eighth is mostly turnip, and has since been given a sword, which it is holding by the wrong end. The children are very pleased with it. So, I think, is he.

The fields go round three sides of the village. The flag is in its row. The sign has not needed amending. The rosette is on Elspeth's stone. And every morning since the morning after, there have still been turnips, and Thistlewick has got up, and looked, and been satisfied, and gone out to them.

<div class="marginalia">I have written a good many articles in this volume on things that tried to end the world, and things that tried to save it, and one long article on the contract that made both of them necessary. This is the only one I have written on something that was never in any danger at all, and I find, having finished it, that it is the one I most wanted to write. I do not understand turnips. I have tried, for two years, and I have not got anywhere. But I think I understand now why a village would send forty-eight of its own north to keep them, and why the forty-eighth would come home and grow them, and why the Dark Lord, of all people, would tell him to. - H.B.</div>
